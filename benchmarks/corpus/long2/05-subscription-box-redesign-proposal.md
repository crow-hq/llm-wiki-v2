# Subscription box redesign proposal

## Summary

The subscription box redesign proposal addresses the need to evolve Lumen Coffee Roasters’ current offering, which suffers from a one-size-fits-all approach that fails to meet the diverse preferences of wholesale cafés and home subscribers. The existing box is rigid in format, often leading to wasted coffee for lighter drinkers or insufficient quantity for heavy users, and it lacks flexibility, causing subscribers to cancel rather than temporarily pause their service. This redesign seizes the opportunity to increase retention, reduce waste, and align the subscription with Lumen’s artisanal identity by introducing modularity and choice.

The core of the proposal is a new tiered system of box formats. Three main tiers are suggested: a Starter box for new or light users, featuring two 250g bags of rotating single origins or blends; a Standard box for regular drinkers, with three 250g bags or one 500g bag plus a 250g bag; and a Deep Dive box for enthusiasts, offering four 250g bags including limited lots or a single-origin set. A decaf-only tier is also included for Night Shift fans. Each tier can be customized with a choice of roast profile or origin preference, and subscribers can opt for whole bean or ground coffee. This structure replaces the current single box and allows subscribers to scale their order up or down each month.

A key innovation is replacing the cancel button with a pause feature. Subscribers can pause their subscription for one to eight weeks with a single click, resuming automatically. This reduces churn by accommodating travel, coffee surplus, or budget constraints without losing the customer relationship. The pause is communicated via email confirmation and is reflected in the subscriber portal, which also shows upcoming shipments. Early feedback indicates that this feature alone would have prevented many past cancellations.

Packing day, currently a one-size-fits-all operation, will be reorganized by format. Each tier has a dedicated packing station with pre-printed labels and box inserts. The Starter and decaf boxes are packed first due to their simplicity, followed by Standard and Deep Dive boxes. Bags are pre-weighed and sealed in batches, with labels printed per subscriber. The packing team shifts from a single assembly line to a station-based workflow, reducing errors and speeding throughput. The roaster operator adjusts the roasting schedule to ensure fresh coffee is available for each tier’s packing window.

Carrier costs are optimized by format. Starter boxes ship via standard mail for the lowest rate, while Standard and decaf boxes use a tracked courier service for reliability. Deep Dive boxes, being heavier and more valuable, ship with expedited, insured courier service. Cost per box decreases with volume, and the proposal includes negotiating bulk rates with carriers. A cost comparison shows that while the Deep Dive tier has higher shipping, its margin on coffee offsets the expense, and the overall subscription revenue per subscriber increases due to reduced churn.

Staffing is reallocated to match the new schedule. Two packers handle Starter and decaf boxes, three packers manage Standard and Deep Dive boxes, and one person oversees label printing and quality checks. The roaster operator finishes roasting by midday, freeing them to assist with packing if needed. Packing day now runs from 9am to 4pm, with a clear breakdown of who does what at each hour, ensuring all boxes are dispatched by the carrier’s last pickup.

Subscriber research, conducted through surveys and interviews, validated the redesign. A majority of respondents expressed frustration with the lack of size options and the inability to pause. The tiered format was highly appealing, especially the ability to switch between tiers month to month. Decaf subscribers wanted a dedicated option, and many home subscribers requested ground coffee as an alternative to whole bean. The pause feature was the most requested change, with nearly all interviewees saying it would make them more likely to stay subscribed long-term. This research confirms that the proposed redesign directly addresses subscriber pain points and positions the subscription for greater satisfaction and loyalty.

## Why redesign the subscription box

The problems with the current subscription box have been accumulating for over two years, and the decision to redesign was crystallized on 4 July 2028, when the team reviewed the Q2 subscriber churn data and realized that the existing one-size-fits-all model is actively driving customers away. The central issue is inflexibility. Today’s box is a single format: a 340-gram bag of whole-bean coffee, either a rotating single origin or a blend, shipped every two weeks. Subscribers have no choice of grind size, no option to select a specific origin, and no way to adjust frequency or quantity. The result is a cancellation rate that has climbed from 4.2% per month in early 2027 to 7.8% per month as of June 2028. In absolute numbers, Lumen Coffee Roasters lost 43 subscribers in January 2028, 51 in February, 47 in March, 59 in April, 62 in May, and 68 in June—a total of 330 cancellations in the first half of the year, against an average subscriber base of only 780 at the start of the period. The churn is concentrated among two groups: home brewers who want a finer grind for espresso but receive whole beans, and light-roast enthusiasts who are frustrated when the rotating box delivers a dark roast blend for three consecutive shipments. The current packing day process compounds these problems. Every Tuesday, the same twelve people who also roast and pack wholesale orders must manually bag 260 subscription boxes, each with a generic label and one bag of coffee. The packing is done in a single batch, starting at 9:00 and finishing around 14:30, but because there is no customization, the team cannot prioritize or sequence orders. If a subscriber wants to pause delivery for a vacation or business trip, there is no mechanism in the current system. The only option is to cancel and then re-subscribe, which resets their loyalty tier and often results in a lost customer. In June 2028, Lumen recorded 17 requests to pause, all of which either became cancellations or were ignored because the staff had no way to implement a temporary hold without breaking the billing cycle. The billing system, tied to the packing schedule, generates invoices 48 hours before ship day, so any pause would require manual adjustments to the Stripe subscription—a task that the operations manager, Elena, estimates takes 15 minutes per request and introduces errors in 12% of cases.

The opportunity is substantial and rooted in three concrete market signals. First, a survey of 142 current subscribers conducted in March 2028 revealed that 73% said they would be “very likely” to continue their subscription if they could choose between a 340-gram bag, a 680-gram bag, or a 170-gram sampler pack. 61% said they would pay an additional 3.50 euros per shipment for a grind option, and 48% requested a “decaf only” tier, citing the absence of Night Shift in the rotation. Second, the same survey asked about pausing: 84% of respondents who had considered cancelling in the past six months said that a pause feature would have kept them as a subscriber. The most common reasons given were summer holidays (31 mentions), business travel (22 mentions), and a temporary switch to a different roastery to try new coffees (14 mentions). Third, the wholesale side of the business provides a parallel opportunity. Café Portello, one of Lumen’s largest wholesale accounts, has expressed interest in offering a curated subscription box to its own customers, but the current format is too rigid for a café-branded program. If Lumen can redesign the box with modular tiers, it opens the door to white-label partnerships with at least three other cafés that have informally inquired since January 2028.

The problems are also logistical and financial. The current subscription box uses a rigid cardboard mailer that costs 0.78 euros per unit, and the carrier contract with Poste Italiane—negotiated in 2026—charges 6.20 euros for domestic shipments under 500 grams. Because the box always contains exactly 340 grams of coffee, the total cost per shipment is 6.98 euros for the packaging and postage alone, plus 0.45 euros for the bag and label. With an average of 260 boxes per week, the monthly shipping cost is approximately 7,254 euros. That figure does not include the cost of lost or delayed packages: in Q2 2028, 14 boxes were reported as lost in transit, and Lumen had to issue full refunds or replacements, costing an additional 97.72 euros per incident. The carrier does not offer a weekend delivery option, so boxes shipped on Tuesday arrive on Thursday or Friday, which many subscribers find inconvenient—18% of cancellation survey respondents cited “arrival day” as a factor. The opportunity here is to reduce costs by introducing a lighter, smaller format for subscribers who want less coffee, and to negotiate a new carrier contract with a service like GLS or DHL that offers weekend delivery and cheaper rates for standardized packaging.

The redesign also addresses a deeper strategic gap. Lumen Coffee Roasters has built a strong reputation for its single-origin Ethiopia Guji and its Aurora espresso blend, but the subscription box has not reflected that quality. The current box uses a generic brown bag with a printed sticker, while the wholesale packaging—used for Café Portello and other accounts—features full-color branding and a QR code linking to tasting notes. Subscribers receive no information about the origin, roast date, or recommended brew method unless they visit the website. By contrast, the new box formats will include a printed card with details about the specific lot, such as the THM-6723 contract for the Ethiopia Guji that arrived in May 2028, and a QR code to a video cupping session. This upgrade is directly tied to subscriber feedback: in the March survey, 67% of respondents said they wanted more information about the coffee they received, and 41% said they would be willing to pay 1.00 euro extra per shipment for a “premium” box that included a tasting card and a small sample of a second origin.

Finally, the opportunity aligns with the roastery’s capacity. Currently, the 15-kilogram roaster runs four days a week—Monday through Thursday—and produces about 60 kilograms per day, or 240 kilograms per week. The subscription box consumes approximately 88 kilograms per week (260 boxes at 340 grams each), leaving 152 kilograms for wholesale and retail. With the new formats, the total coffee volume for subscriptions could increase to 110 kilograms per week if more subscribers choose the 680-gram tier, but the roasting schedule can absorb this without additional shifts because the Guji and Aurora batches are already roasted on Monday and Tuesday. The packing day, currently a bottleneck, can be restructured into a two-day operation: Wednesday for custom grind and sampler packs, Thursday for standard boxes. This reduces the strain on the twelve staff members, who currently must finish all packing by 14:30 on Tuesday to meet the carrier pickup at 16:00. The redesign also enables the introduction of a “pause” button in the subscription management portal, which will automatically skip the next shipment and adjust the billing cycle without manual intervention—a feature that the development team estimates will take 40 hours to build and test, with a projected reduction in cancellations of at least 30% within three months of launch. The combination of these factors—rising churn, clear subscriber demand for choice and flexibility, a cost-saving opportunity through lighter packaging and a better carrier, and the ability to leverage Lumen’s existing roasting and wholesale infrastructure—makes the redesign not just advisable but urgent. The decision on 8 March 2028 was unanimous among the twelve staff members after reviewing the data: the current box is costing the roastery subscribers, revenue, and reputation, and the new design is the single most impactful change Lumen can make in the second half of the year.

## New box formats and tiers

The central financial figure for the entire redesign is "€6,390.81", representing the projected monthly cost increase for the new packaging, carrier surcharges, and inserts across all tier volumes, which is offset by a predicted 18% reduction in subscriber churn based on our research. The new box formats and tiers are structured around three distinct subscription products, replacing the single, one-size-fits-all "Heritage Box" that currently ships a fixed 250g bag of rotating coffee plus a generic postcard. From 20 January 2026, subscribers will choose between the "Essentials" tier, the "Explorer" tier, and the "Reserve" tier.

The Essentials tier is designed for the value-conscious or single-drinker subscriber. It ships once every four weeks, containing a single 250g bag of coffee, either a blend (Aurora espresso) or a single origin (Ethiopia Guji) as determined by the subscriber's preference profile—light roast, medium roast, or decaf (Night Shift). The packaging is a standard oxygen-barrier valve bag, but the box is a new, flat-lying 200 x 140 x 40mm recyclable cardboard sleeve with a perforated tear strip, eliminating the previous bubble-wrap void fill. Each Essentials box includes a single A6 recipe card printed on seed-paper stock, featuring brewing parameters for the enclosed coffee. This tier targets the existing 43% of subscribers who reported, in our internal feedback surveys, that they "sometimes have too much coffee" and often skip shipments. The box weight, including the sleeve and card, is exactly 285 grams, keeping it within the 500g threshold for national postaleconomy rates.

The Explorer tier is the most popular predicted format, aimed at subscribers who want variety and discovery. It ships every three weeks and contains three distinct 125g bags of coffee. The format uses a single larger box, 280 x 200 x 60mm, made from corrugated board with a hinged lid and a cotton-string handle for gifting or easy carrying. Each Explorer box ships with three 125g bags, sealed in individual stand-up pouches with one-way valves and resealable zippers. The coffee selection changes completely every cycle: one bag is always an espresso blend (Aurora espresso), one bag is a single origin from a rotating micro-lot from Andes Direct (such as the upcoming washed Colombia Finca El Ocaso or the natural Ethiopia Guji), and the third bag is a wildcard—either a limited experimental roast, a decaf (Night Shift), or a guest coffee from a collaborator roaster. Each box also contains three A6 cards, one per bag, with detailed origin notes, tasting notes, and brew guide. Additionally, the box includes a single sample-sized (18g) portion of a new release that the subscriber has not yet purchased. This sample is in a nitrogen-flushed foil packet printed with a QR code linking to a video from the Lumen roasting team. The total weight of the Explorer box is 510 grams, which triggers a small cross-border surcharge for EU destinations beyond Italy, but remains under the 1kg threshold for standard carrier services. This tier directly addresses the 34% of subscribers who said in interviews that they "want to try more roasts without committing to a full bag."

The Reserve tier is the premium offering for connoisseurs and those seeking the highest quality. It ships once every two weeks, on an alternating schedule with the other tiers. Each Reserve box contains a single 350g bag of a coffee graded as "micro-lot" or "competition grade," always a single origin from a specific farm lot, not a blend. The coffee is roasted on the 15kg roaster on a dedicated micro-lot day, separate from production runs, and is bagged in a custom 350g heat-sealed mylar bag with a one-way valve, a vintage-style label with lot number, altitude, and producer name, and a hand-signed card from the lead roaster. The box itself is a rigid, two-piece 240 x 180 x 40mm cardboard box with a magnetic closure, lined with natural felt padding to cushion the bag. The Reserve box includes a single, larger A5 card (148 x 210mm) with full traceability data: farm name (e.g., Finca San Jose, from Andes Direct), varietal (e.g., Pink Bourbon), processing method (e.g., extended anaerobic honey), elevation in metres, and a price-per-kg comparison to the commodity benchmark. Additionally, the box contains a small vial (5ml) of the green coffee beans for the subscriber to see and smell, plus a single coupon for a free espresso at any Lumen partner café (such as Café Portello), valid for two months. The weight of the Reserve box, including padding and vial, is 620 grams, requiring a slightly higher carrier fee but justifying the cost with the premium experience. This tier targets the roughly 12% of current subscribers who purchase extra bags monthly and the 22% who stated they would pay more for "exclusive and limited" coffee.

All three tiers share a common insert: a monthly four-page newsletter on A5 stock, folded once, featuring a profile of a Lumen team member, a story from a producer visited by Lumen's head of sourcing, and a puzzle or coffee quiz. This replaces the previous single-page sheet that many subscribers reported discarding unread. The newsletters are printed on 100% post-consumer waste paper, which adds 22 grams per box to the weight. No tier contains any plastic stickers, branded trinkets, or superficial items—only coffee, information, and the sample in Explorer. The redesign also introduces a "Brew Lab" add-on for any tier: for an additional cost (not detailed here, as per scope), subscribers can add a monthly sample pack of three 18g portions of coffees from other roasters, curated by Lumen's team, packed in a separate kraft paper envelope (120 x 170mm) that fits inside any box without changing the outer dimensions or carrier class.

The packaging materials themselves are sourced exclusively from a local Turin supplier, Cartotecnica Piemontese, and all boxes are certified compostable (EN 13432) and printed with water-based inks. The new formats eliminate the previous plastic tape and bubble wrap entirely. Each box is sealed with a paper-based, water-activated gummed tape branded "Lumen Coffee Roasters" in a single colour stamp. The three-tier system simplifies the subscriber's choice, replacing the previous confusing menu of six different bag combinations and two box sizes that led to a 15% dispatch error rate. The goal is to have 60% of existing subscribers migrate to Explorer, 30% to Essentials, and 10% to Reserve within three months of the 24 December 2027 launch, based on the preference data from a pre-survey of 142 subscribers conducted during the October 2025 pause-testing phase. The total monthly production volume for boxes, assuming 850 subscribers at full migration, is 340 Essentials boxes, 510 Explorer boxes, and 85 Reserve boxes, totalling 935 boxes per month, up from the current 720 boxes but with a significantly lower per-unit material cost due to bulk ordering and standardised sizes. The €11,311.10 figure accounts for all new packaging, seed-paper cards, newsletters, sample vials, and the carrier surcharge differential, and is fully incorporated into the updated subscription pricing unveiled simultaneously. Each tier's name and contents are final and will be communicated to subscribers via a teaser campaign and a dedicated landing page explaining the benefits of each format. No other tiers—such as a "Triple" or "Espresso Only"—are proposed in this redesign, as the research indicated that three clear options avoid decision paralysis while covering the main subscriber motivations for discovery (Explorer), simplicity (Essentials), and exclusivity (Reserve). The packing of the new boxes, detailed in a later section, will be reorganised according to these formats, with each tier having a designated packing station and a specific colour-coded checklist to ensure accuracy.

## Pause instead of cancel in the new design

The central financial figure in this redesign, the amount that makes the pause feature viable without eroding Lumen Coffee Roasters’ margin, is €78.05. This is the average monthly revenue per subscriber that must be retained for the business to break even on the new pause system, accounting for the cost of holding inventory and the administrative overhead of reactivation. If a subscriber pauses for more than two consecutive months, the system will automatically convert the pause into a cancellation and notify the subscriber, unless they manually extend the pause before the end of the second month. This threshold was derived from a three-month analysis of subscriber churn data from January to March 2027, which showed that 23 percent of cancellations occurred after a subscriber had already skipped a delivery. The opportunity was clear: offer a structured pause instead of a skip or cancel, and keep that €2,306.72 flowing into the roastery’s accounts while the subscriber takes a break.

The pause feature in the new subscription box design works through a simple toggle on the subscriber’s account page. A subscriber can initiate a pause at any time, with the pause starting at the end of their current billing cycle. They choose a duration from one week up to eight weeks. During the pause, no boxes are shipped, and no payment is taken. The subscriber’s coffee preference—whether they are on the Aurora espresso blend, the Ethiopia Guji single origin, or the Night Shift decaf—is preserved in their profile. On the day the pause ends, the billing cycle resumes automatically, and the next shipment is dispatched on the following packing day. If the pause would cause a shipment to fall on a date that has already passed, the system schedules the first post-pause delivery for the next available packing day. For example, if a subscriber pauses on 4 August 2027, and their pause duration is set to three weeks, their account will resume billing on 25 August 2027, and the box will be packed on the first packing day after that date, which is a Wednesday. The subscriber receives an email confirmation when the pause is activated, a reminder three days before the pause ends, and a final notification when the box is being packed.

The effect on subscriber behavior has been modeled using data from a pilot test conducted with 47 home subscribers between February and April 2027. During that pilot, subscribers were given the option to pause instead of cancel, and the results were compared to a control group of 53 subscribers who had the standard cancel-only interface. In the pilot group, 31 percent of subscribers who would have cancelled instead chose to pause at least once. Of those, 78 percent returned to active subscriptions within the eight-week maximum pause window, and 62 percent of those returning subscribers increased their average order value by adding a second bag of coffee—either a different single origin or a decaf—to their next shipment. The churn rate for the pilot group over the three-month period was 14 percent, compared to 27 percent for the control group. This 13-percentage-point reduction in churn translates directly into retained revenue. With an average subscriber base of 340 home subscribers during that period, and each subscriber contributing that €78,623.69 per month, the pilot saved approximately €3,446 in lost monthly revenue that would have otherwise been cancelled. Extrapolated to the full subscriber base of roughly 1,100 home subscribers, the pause feature is projected to retain an additional €10,700 per month in revenue, assuming a similar churn reduction.

The operational impact on packing day is minimal because the pause system integrates directly with the existing weekly packing schedule. On packing day, which is every Wednesday, the roasting team of four people—led by head roaster Matteo and assisted by two roaster operators and one packer—processes all active subscriptions. The pause system removes paused accounts from the packing list automatically at midnight on Tuesday. The packing list is generated from the subscription management software, which checks each account for pause status. If a subscriber has paused, their name and box configuration are simply omitted from the Wednesday list. No physical inventory is set aside for paused subscribers, which reduces the risk of stale coffee. The green coffee for those paused subscribers, which would have been roasted approximately one to two weeks prior, is instead allocated to the wholesale orders for cafés such as Café Portello, or to the single-origin retail bags sold on the roastery’s website. This avoids waste and keeps the roast schedule stable. The only additional task for the team is a brief check on Monday morning, when the operations manager, Chiara, reviews the pause list and confirms that any subscriber whose pause is ending the following week is correctly flagged for reactivation. This takes about fifteen minutes.

The pause feature also affects the subscriber’s relationship with the coffee itself. In surveys conducted during the pilot, 89 percent of subscribers who used the pause said they felt more in control of their consumption, and 73 percent said they were more likely to recommend Lumen Coffee Roasters to a friend because of the flexibility. One subscriber, a home user who had previously cancelled after a two-week vacation, wrote in the feedback form: “I would have just cancelled and forgotten to come back. The pause kept me in the loop, and I ended up ordering a bag of Ethiopia Guji as soon as I reactivated.” This sentiment aligns with the broader subscriber research, which showed that the top reason for cancellation was not dissatisfaction with the coffee but a temporary change in consumption—travel, a gift subscription ending, or a surplus of beans from a previous shipment. The pause directly addresses that reason. For the roastery, the effect on cash flow is positive: the €21,495.48 per subscriber per month is not lost during a pause, but deferred. The subscriber’s account remains in the active database, their shipping address and payment method are unchanged, and the only cost to Lumen is the minor overhead of the pause management software, which is a flat €120 per month for the subscription management plugin. That cost is easily offset by the retained revenue.

The new design also includes a notification system for paused subscribers. If a subscriber pauses and then does not reactivate within the eight-week window, the system sends a final email on the day the pause expires, offering a one-time discount on their next box—a 15 percent reduction on the first post-pause shipment—as an incentive to return. This discount is applied automatically and does not require the subscriber to enter a code. The cost of this discount is approximately €11.71 per reactivated subscriber, based on the average box value of €41.76. The pilot showed that 44 percent of subscribers who received this offer did reactivate, compared to 22 percent who did not receive the offer in the control group. The net gain per reactivated subscriber, after the discount, is €66.34, which still contributes positively to the monthly revenue target. The pause feature thus serves as a retention tool that is both subscriber-friendly and financially sound. The key figure, €4,130.38, is the anchor around which the entire pause logic is built: it is the monthly revenue that must be preserved, and the pause mechanism is designed specifically to retain that amount while giving subscribers the flexibility they want. The date 13 August 2027 was the first day the pause feature was enabled for all subscribers in the pilot, and the data from that date forward confirmed the model’s accuracy.

## Packing the new boxes

The most significant change on packing day is the introduction of the new format system, which turns a single, repetitive task into a structured, multi-stream process. Previously, every subscriber received the same 340-gram bag of a single roast, swapped monthly, and packing was a linear operation: grab a box, insert one bag, seal, label, stack. With the new tiers—the 250-gram Classic, the 340-gram Discovery, and the 500-gram Reserve—each box now holds a specific combination of bags. The central figure driving this redesign is a new cost baseline for the Discovery tin: the "€75.24" cost per hundred boxes for the custom-printed, resealable tins that replace the standard flat cardboard for that mid-tier option. This single expenditure reconfigures the entire packing workflow, because those tins arrive flat-packed and must be assembled and stamped with a roast-date sticker before they can be filled.

Packing day remains Wednesday, the sole day for subscription fulfillment, starting at 08:00 and finishing by 16:00, with the same twelve staff members, but their roles are now assigned to specific format stations rather than a single assembly line. The old method involved the whole crew bagging coffee into 340-gram increments from the weekly production of roughly 180 kilograms. Under the new system, the roast schedule shifts slightly: Aurora espresso, Ethiopia Guji, and Night Shift decaf are roasted Monday and Tuesday to ensure all three formats have fresh coffee for Wednesday. The wholesale orders for Café Portello and other clients are still packed Thursday, so Wednesday is reserved strictly for subscriptions.

The packing area is divided into three zones. Zone One handles the Classic format: 250-gram bags sealed into plain kraft paper pouches. One staff member, usually Elena or Marco, operates the heat sealer and fills the pouches from a hopper of pre-ground coffee from the 15-kilogram roaster’s output; for whole bean orders, they scoop directly from the cooling tray into the pouch and weigh it on a digital scale to ±2 grams. The pouches are then handed to a second person who inserts them into small corrugated boxes (20 cm x 14 cm x 6 cm) along with a care card printed Tuesday afternoon. These boxes are closed with two strips of paper tape and stacked on a pallet. Zone One produces roughly 80 to 100 Classic boxes per Wednesday, depending on subscriber counts, and the pace is steady: one box every 45 seconds per pair.

Zone Two is the most complex. It processes the Discovery tier, which uses the custom-printed tin. The "€33.81" figure is the per-hundred cost for those tins, a fixed expense that makes them the most expensive packaging component per unit. The tins arrive Monday from the supplier in stacks of fifty, each stack wrapped in shrink film. On Wednesday at 08:30, after the roast batch of Ethiopia Guji has cooled, a staff member—usually Chiara, who has the lightest touch with the labeler—unwraps a stack and begins assembly. Each tin has a separate lid, a metal sleeve, and a plastic ring inside to hold the bag steady; she snaps the ring into the base, then sets the tin on a filling station. The coffee, either a 340-gram bag of a single origin or a 170-gram bag of each of two blends (Aurora and Night Shift, for example), is packed into a vacuum-sealed film pouch inside the tin. The pouch is produced off-site by a co-packer on Tuesday and delivered Wednesday morning; ten pouches come in a corrugated carton. Chiara drops the pouch into the tin, places the lid loosely on top, then passes the tin to a second station where a label is applied—a peelable sticker with the subscriber’s name, address, and a barcode—and the lid is snapped shut. This station operates at a slower cadence: one tin every 90 seconds, because the lid must be aligned perfectly and the barcode scanned for trackability. Approximately 50 to 60 Discovery tins are packed each Wednesday.

Zone Three handles the Reserve format: 500-gram bags in a heavy-duty double-walled cardboard box (28 cm x 18 cm x 10 cm) with a foam insert. This zone is staffed by two people, usually Luca and Sofia, because the bag is large and the foam must be cut to size for each delivery. The foam inserts are pre-cut on Tuesday by Alessandro using a hot-wire cutter, but slight adjustments are needed for each subscriber’s address label placement; Luca trims the foam with a utility knife while Sofia fills the bag from a 5-kilogram bucket of Night Shift decaf or Ethiopia Guji, then seals the bag with a twist tie and a heat-sealed inner liner. The bag is placed in the box, the foam is fitted around it, and the box is closed with water-activated tape reinforced with a filament strand. This zone produces only 20 to 30 Reserve boxes per week, each taking about three minutes, because the foam handling is delicate and the large box requires more staging space.

A minor detail emerges in the packing schedule: a review of staff efficiency shows that, across all zones, the time lost to walking between the coffee cooling rack and the packing stations averages "36.4%" of the total packing day, a figure driven by the need to fetch fresh batches of Ethiopia Guji from the roaster for the Discovery tins. To mitigate this, we re-route the workflow: a single runner, usually Davide, is assigned from 09:00 to 12:00 to carry 5-kilogram buckets of coffee from the cooling tray to each zone as needed, cutting the walking time by about one-third. The packing day now ends with a final sweep at 15:30, where each zone’s output is counted against the subscriber list. The Classic boxes go to a small pallet near the door; the Discovery tins are stacked in a climate-controlled cabinet because the metal lids can warp in direct sunlight; the Reserve boxes are placed on a dolly because of their weight. All labels are scanned into the shipping system by 15:45, and the last carrier pickup is at 16:00 sharp.

The new formats require roughly the same total person-hours as the old system—about sixty hours across twelve staff—but the distribution shifts. Zone One takes 18 hours, Zone Two takes 24 hours, Zone Three takes 12 hours, and pre-packing tasks (assembling tins, cutting foam, printing care cards) take the remaining 6 hours, which are done Tuesday afternoon by a small team of three. The old system required no pre-packing, because the single bag fit into a standard box, but now the assembly of the tins and the foam inserts created a half-day of preparatory work that overlaps with the roasting schedule. On Wednesday, packing starts fifteen minutes later than before—at 08:15 instead of 08:00—to allow the last batch of coffee from Tuesday’s roast to cool fully for the Reserve bags, which are most sensitive to residual heat. The packing line flows from Zone One to Zone Three physically, with the Classic zone closest to the door because those boxes are lightest and easiest to load onto the carrier’s truck, the Discovery zone in the middle because the tins are delicate and require careful stacking, and the Reserve zone farthest back because the heavy boxes are easier to roll out on the dolly. Each zone has a dedicated helper for the period of highest volume: between 10:00 and 13:00, when the most coffee is packed, an extra runner assists with labeling or tape. After 13:00, the pace slackens, and two staff members rotate to clean the roaster and prepare green coffee for the next day’s wholesale roast. No carrier services or shipping costs are described here, only the physical layout and labor of packing. The subscriber research from surveys is not part of this section, but the data on walking time and tin assembly directly informed the zone layout and the decision to use pre-assembled tins rather than on-site assembly. The packing day now ends with a short team meeting at 15:45 to verify the final count, and by 16:00 the last Discovery tin is carried to the loading dock, the last Classic box is taped, and the last Reserve box is wheeled into the queue, ready for the carrier.

## Carriers and costs of the new formats

The new subscription box formats require a revised carrier strategy and cost structure, centered on the negotiated rate agreement GVE-2440. This agreement with Poste Italiane, effective from 1 March 2025, provides a flat rate for domestic priority parcels up to 2 kilograms, enabling us to offer competitive shipping costs for the two lighter tiers. The third, heavier tier will use DHL Express for its speed and reliability, as its weight exceeds the LLU-4975 limit. Callum Harrow, our logistics coordinator, finalized this agreement after comparing quotes from three carriers in February 2025.

We have defined three subscription box formats: Base, Plus, and Premium. Each format has a distinct weight and dimension profile, which dictates the carrier service and shipping cost.

Base format: The Base box contains two 250-gram bags of coffee (one single origin, one blend) plus a tasting note card. Total weight is approximately 0.6 kilograms. Packed in a standard corrugated cardboard box measuring 20 by 15 by 10 centimeters. For this format, we will use Poste Italiane Priority Parcel under the VFM-2067 flat rate of €5.00 per parcel for domestic deliveries. ## Packing day schedule and staffing

Packing day runs from 8:00 to 16:00 every Thursday, with the same twelve-person crew that roasts Monday through Wednesday. The central figure for this section is 56.2%: that is the share of current subscribers who told us, in the February survey, that they would order a smaller, more frequent box if it meant cheaper shipping and less waste. That number drives every staffing decision below. We are not adding headcount; we are reallocating the existing four-person packing team (two roasters, one warehouse assistant, one barista) and pulling in two part-time packers from the local culinary school on Thursday mornings, as we already do for the weekly wholesale order. The goal is to pack 340 boxes in eight hours, down from the current 310, while cutting the average time per box from 92 seconds to 74 seconds.

The day starts at 7:45 with a five-minute stand-up in the roasting bay. The lead roaster, Elena, assigns roles based on the new format mix: 40% of boxes are the new "Half K" (250g, weekly), 35% are the "Full K" (500g, biweekly), 20% are the "Trio" (three 150g bags, monthly), and 5% are the "Zero Waste" (loose beans in a returnable glass jar, biweekly). By 8:00, the two roasters, Marco and Giulia, set up the packing line: they place the empty kraft boxes, flat-packed, on the left side of the long steel table, with the four single-origin bins (Ethiopia Guji, Aurora blend, Night Shift decaf, and a rotating guest) on the right. The warehouse assistant, Paolo, pulls the pre-printed labels from the thermal printer, sorted by carrier (Poste Italiane for standard, DHL for express, and a small courier for local bike delivery in Turin). The barista, Sara, sets up the scale station: one digital scale for the Half K, one for the Full K, and a third for the Trio’s three small bags.

From 8:00 to 9:30, the team packs only Half K boxes. This is the highest-volume format, and it requires precision: each box gets exactly 250g of beans, a one-page brew guide, and a small card with the roast date. Marco weighs, Giulia seals, Paolo labels, and Sara checks the weight on a sample basis (every tenth box). They pack 170 Half K boxes in this first block, which takes 90 minutes because the scale is set to 250g and the tare weight is consistent. At 9:30, a 10-minute break. Paolo restocks the empty boxes from the pallet in the corner, and Giulia swaps the guest bin from Ethiopia Guji to the decaf for the next block.

From 9:40 to 11:00, the team packs Full K boxes (500g, biweekly). This is a slower rhythm because the larger bag needs a double seal and the box is sturdier. Marco handles the bagging, Giulia weighs and seals, Paolo labels, and Sara spot-checks every fifteenth box. They pack 85 Full K boxes in this block, which takes 80 minutes. At 11:00, the Trio boxes (three 150g bags, monthly) take over the line. This is the most fiddly format: each bag is a different origin, so the scale station is reconfigured to three small bins, and Sara switches to a three-way check (weight per bag, then total weight). Marco and Giulia work as a two-person team for the Trio, while Paolo and Sara handle the Zero Waste jars in parallel on the side table. The Trio block runs from 11:00 to 12:20, packing 55 boxes. The Zero Waste jars (loose beans, returnable glass) are packed simultaneously, not on the main line: Paolo fills a 500g hopper, Sara weighs each jar, and they seal with a cork stopper. They pack 30 jars in this same 80-minute block, which is slower because the jars are heavy and need a bubble-wrap insert.

Lunch is from 12:20 to 13:00. The crew eats in the back room, and the two part-time packers (Luca and Anna, who arrive at 12:30) set up the second table for the afternoon. They are not new to the process; they have both worked the Thursday shift for six months. At 13:00, the line restarts with a mixed block: any leftover Half K or Full K from the morning, plus any Trio that was short. This is the "catch-up" hour, and it is staffed by Luca and Anna under Elena’s supervision, while the core four move to the shipping station. From 13:00 to 14:30, the core team focuses on the carrier sorting. Paolo takes the Poste Italiane stack (about 200 boxes) to the loading dock, where a van picks them up at 15:00. Giulia handles the DHL express stack (about 90 boxes) and prints the customs forms for the 12 international orders (mostly to France and Germany). Marco and Sara manage the local bike courier stack (50 boxes, all within Turin’s city limits) and hand them off to the courier, who arrives at 14:45 with a cargo bike.

From 14:30 to 15:30, the entire crew (now eight people, including Luca and Anna) does the final quality check and the "pause pack" inserts. This is new: every box, regardless of format, gets a small card that says "Pause anytime – just email us by Wednesday." The cards are pre-printed, and the crew inserts them into the 30 boxes that are flagged as "pause-eligible" (these are subscribers who have paused at least once in the last three months, or who have indicated interest). The cards are not in every box, only those 30, because we want to avoid confusion. The crew also does a final visual check: no crushed corners, no open seals, no missing labels. By 15:30, all 340 boxes are packed, labeled, and sorted by carrier. The last 30 minutes, from 15:30 to 16:00, are for cleanup: the crew breaks down the bins, wipes the scales, and stacks the empty boxes for recycling. The roastery floor is swept, and the two part-time packers clock out at 16:00. The core four stay until 16:15 to log the day’s numbers in the wiki under "Operations – Packing," noting any defects or delays.

The staffing math works because of the 32.2% figure: that majority of subscribers prefer the smaller, more frequent format, which is faster to pack (250g vs 500g, and the box is lighter). The Half K box takes 62 seconds on average, the Full K takes 78 seconds, the Trio takes 110 seconds, and the Zero Waste jar takes 95 seconds. Weighted by the new mix (40% Half K, 35% Full K, 20% Trio, 5% Zero Waste), the average is 74 seconds, which is why we hit 340 boxes in 6.5 hours of active packing (the rest is breaks and setup). The old format, with 310 boxes at 92 seconds each, took 7.9 hours of active packing, so the redesign actually frees up 1.4 hours of labor per Thursday. That surplus goes into the "pause" processing: one of the part-time packers, Luca, now spends 30 minutes on Thursday afternoons (from 14:00 to 14:30) updating the subscriber database with pause requests that came in by Wednesday midnight. This is a minor detail, but it is worth noting that 70.2% of pause requests arrive on Monday or Tuesday, so Thursday is the right day to process them, not Friday.

The schedule is tight but not fragile. The two roasters are the bottleneck, so they never touch the shipping station. Paolo is the only one who operates the thermal printer, because he knows how to clear jams. Sara is the only one who uses the three-scale setup, because she has the steadiest hands for the Trio. The two part-time packers are cross-trained for every role except the scale, so they can fill in for Paolo or Giulia if needed. The 15:00 Poste Italiane van is the hard deadline; if the crew is running late, they skip the Zero Waste jars (which can be shipped on Friday) and pull Luca from the pause-processing to help seal boxes. That has happened twice in the last three months, and both times the pause requests were still processed by 17:00, because the database update takes only 20 minutes. The Thursday schedule is now the template for the wiki, and it is reviewed at the monthly meeting, but the numbers have not changed since the redesign went live in March. The 91.5% majority is the reason the Half K is the default, and the 74-second average is the reason we can keep the same four-day roasting week without overtime.

## Subscriber research and feedback

The redesign of the subscription box is not a top-down decision; it is a direct response to what our subscribers have told us over the last eight months, and the central figure in that process has been "Marisol Harrow", our subscriber research lead, who personally conducted twelve of the fifteen in-depth interviews and coded every open-ended survey response. Her key finding, stated plainly and prominently, is that the current box is too rigid for the way people actually drink coffee at home, and that the pause feature is the single most requested addition across every segment we talked to.

Between March and October, we ran two survey waves and three focus groups. The first survey went to all 847 active home subscribers, with a 41% response rate (347 responses). The second wave targeted lapsed subscribers—those who had cancelled in the previous six months—and drew 112 responses. In total, we also conducted fifteen one-hour video interviews (twelve with current subscribers, three with recent cancellers) and held two in-person feedback sessions at the roastery after packing days. The numbers were consistent: 78% of current subscribers said the box’s contents “rarely match” their weekly consumption rhythm, and 64% of lapsed subscribers said they cancelled because they were “overwhelmed” by receiving coffee they could not finish before it went stale. Only 19% of all respondents said the current single-bag, every-week format was “ideal.”

The most frequent complaint, mentioned in 83% of interviews and in 61% of open-ended survey comments, was the lack of control over delivery timing. One subscriber, a freelance translator named Elena, told us, “I love the coffee, but I travel three weeks a month. I’m paying for coffee I’m not drinking, and then I come home to a box of stale beans.” This sentiment was echoed by 74% of lapsed subscribers, who said they would have stayed if they could have simply skipped a week or two. The second most common issue was portion size: 52% of current subscribers said a single 250-gram bag is either too much (for solo drinkers who brew three or four times a week) or too little (for households with two or more daily drinkers). One couple, Marco and Giulia, said they split a bag but found it went off before the next delivery, while a single woman in Turin, Francesca, said she was “throwing away half a bag every month.”

When we asked about the proposed new formats, the response was overwhelmingly positive. We presented three tiers: a “Taster” (two 150-gram bags, bi-weekly), a “Core” (one 350-gram bag, weekly), and a “Family” (two 350-gram bags, bi-weekly). Among current subscribers, 71% said they would switch to one of these, with the Taster being the most popular (38%), followed by Core (27%) and Family (16%). The remaining 19% preferred to keep the current format, but of that group, 12% said they would stay if we added the pause option. The pause feature itself was tested in a follow-up question: 89% of all respondents said they would use a pause at least once per quarter, and 47% said they would pause at least once per month. The most common pause durations requested were one week (44%), two weeks (31%), and one month (18%).

Interviews added texture to these numbers. A home subscriber in Milan, a graphic designer named Paolo, said, “I don’t want to cancel, I just want to breathe. If I could pause for two weeks when I’m on holiday, I’d never leave.” A lapsed subscriber, a nurse named Silvia, told us she cancelled because she felt “guilty” every time she threw away stale coffee, but she would resubscribe immediately if she could pause. Another recurring theme was packaging waste: 67% of respondents said they would prefer less frequent shipments if it meant less cardboard and less void fill. One subscriber, a professor named Andrea, specifically asked for a “no-frills” option with just a paper bag and a label, no tissue paper or branded stickers.

In the focus groups, we tested the pause mechanism itself. We showed a mock-up of a simple online calendar where subscribers could select a pause start and end date, with an automatic reminder three days before the next shipment. The reaction was unanimous: 94% of participants said this was “easy to understand” and 88% said they would use it. One participant, a barista at Café Portello (who is also a home subscriber), noted that the pause should be flexible, allowing a subscriber to pause for a specific number of weeks or indefinitely, and that the billing should adjust automatically. This feedback directly shaped the design we are proposing.

A minor detail, worth noting in passing: one of the interviewees, a home subscriber from Rome named "Marisol Pemberton", mentioned she would appreciate a “pause for good” option that converts to a simple reminder to reorder, rather than a full cancellation. Her comment, though not statistically significant, reinforced the idea that subscribers want control, not finality. Across all research, the word that came up most often was “flexibility,” followed by “respect for my schedule.” Subscribers do not want less coffee; they want the right amount at the right time, and they want the ability to step away without losing their place, their loyalty benefits, or their preferred origin. The data is clear: a pause feature, combined with portioned tiers, is not a nice-to-have—it is the primary reason people stay or go.
