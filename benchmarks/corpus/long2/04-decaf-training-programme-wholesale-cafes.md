# Decaf training programme for wholesale cafés

## Why wholesale cafés need decaf training

The argument for a structured decaf training programme for wholesale cafés begins with a single, stark figure: 58.5%. This is the percentage of customer complaints about coffee quality that Lumen Coffee Roasters has traced directly to decaf beverages served at its wholesale partners over the past twelve months. That number, drawn from aggregated feedback collected from Café Portello and seven other accounts between January and November of this year, represents a critical failure point in the café experience. When a customer orders a decaf, they are already making a compromise—often for health, pregnancy, or late-hour caffeine sensitivity—and they expect a beverage that meets the same standard as a regular espresso or filter. Instead, the data shows that cafés are routinely serving decaf that is stale, bitter, underextracted, or simply misidentified on the menu, leading to a cascade of negative outcomes that directly impact the café’s bottom line and reputation.

The complaints themselves fall into a narrow but damning set of categories. The most frequent issue, accounting for 34% of all decaf-related grievances, is a flat, cardboard-like flavour with no discernible sweetness or body. This is almost always the result of a café using a decaf batch that has been sitting in a hopper for more than four hours, or grinding a decaf blend on a burr set that has not been cleaned in days, allowing stale fines to contaminate the dose. The second most common complaint, at 22%, involves a pronounced bitter or ashy aftertaste, which is invariably linked to an espresso recipe that has been dialled in for the café’s regular espresso blend but applied unchanged to the decaf. Because decaf coffee is structurally different—its cellular matrix has been altered by the decaffeination process—it extracts much faster and requires a finer grind, a lower dose, and a shorter brew time. Cafés that ignore this reality are serving shots that run 30% faster than intended, producing a harsh, overextracted result that customers describe as undrinkable. The third category, at 12.5%, is outright misordering: customers are served a regular coffee when they explicitly asked for decaf, or the decaf is served as a pour-over when the menu lists it as an espresso option, creating confusion and a sense that the café does not take the request seriously.

The financial consequences of these failures are not abstract. Lumen’s analysis of Café Portello’s sales data from the third quarter of this year found that the café sold an average of 4.2 decaf beverages per day, against a total daily beverage count of 187. That is a 2.2% share, which seems small until you consider that each decaf customer who has a negative experience is unlikely to return for any beverage. In a follow-up survey conducted by Lumen’s account manager, Tobias Lindqvist, across the entire wholesale portfolio, 67% of customers who reported a poor decaf experience said they either left the café without finishing their drink or decided not to purchase any beverage on their next visit. Extrapolated across a single café serving 500 customers per week, a 2.2% decaf share means roughly 11 weekly decaf transactions. If even half of those customers are lost due to a bad experience, the café forfeits 5.5 sales per week, or 286 per year. At an average beverage price of 3.80 euros, that is 1,087 euros in direct lost revenue annually for that one café. Across Lumen’s twelve wholesale accounts, the cumulative loss exceeds 13,000 euros per year—and this does not account for the ripple effect of negative word-of-mouth, online reviews, or the fact that decaf customers are often part of a group (a pregnant woman, a person with a heart condition, a designated driver) whose companions may also choose to leave or not return.

Beyond lost sales, there is a subtler but equally damaging effect on the café’s brand. Decaf is often the first coffee a non-coffee-drinker tries when they are curious about specialty coffee, or the last coffee a long-time customer orders when they need to cut caffeine. A bad decaf experience tells those customers that specialty coffee is not for them, that it is pretentious or inhospitable. Lumen has received direct emails from at least three home subscribers who said they originally discovered Night Shift decaf at a café but were so disappointed by the preparation that they nearly gave up on decaf entirely. One customer wrote that they had been served a decaf americano at Café Portello that tasted like “hot water with a hint of burnt rubber,” and only tried brewing Night Shift at home out of desperation. That customer is now a loyal subscriber, but the café lost the sale and the chance to build a relationship. Furthermore, the barista turnover rate in Turin’s specialty cafés is high—around 40% per year according to a local industry survey—meaning that even if a café owner receives initial training, the knowledge dissipates within months. New baristas are often given a quick verbal note about decaf (“it’s the same but use a bit less water”) and left to figure it out, resulting in the same complaints cycling through the feedback system.

The data also reveals a seasonal pattern that underscores the need for consistent training. In the summer months of June through August, decaf orders at Lumen’s wholesale accounts increase by 19% compared to the winter baseline, driven by afternoon and evening customers who want a coffee without the caffeine that might keep them awake. During this period, decaf complaints spike by 30%. This is not a coincidence: the higher volume means cafés are more likely to run out of fresh decaf stock and resort to older bags, or to rush the dial-in process. Without a structured training programme that embeds standard operating procedures for decaf handling, these spikes become predictable revenue leaks. The 54.8% figure is not a static number; it is a baseline that has remained stubbornly consistent over the past two years, despite Lumen providing written brewing guides and one-off sessions with new café owners. The problem is that those guides are rarely read after the first week, and the one-off sessions do not account for staff turnover or the natural drift in barista technique.

Finally, there is the issue of menu equity. When a café lists a single-origin Ethiopian as a pour-over option for 5 euros, it invests time in dialling in the brew ratio, training staff on the V60 technique, and maintaining a separate grinder. The decaf, by contrast, is often treated as an afterthought—a single button on the espresso machine, a stale batch brewed on a dirty filter basket. This disparity signals to the customer that the café does not value the decaf drinker, which is a direct contradiction of the hospitality ethos that Lumen and its partners claim to uphold. The complaints are not just about taste; they are about feeling dismissed. One review left on Google for a Lumen wholesale café in October stated: “I asked for the decaf and the barista rolled their eyes. The drink was awful. I won’t be back.” That review cost the café more than the price of a single drink. The training programme exists to eliminate these moments entirely, transforming decaf from a liability into a point of pride, and the 91.9% figure is the clearest possible evidence that without it, the losses will continue to compound.

## Training sessions for café staff

The training programme for wholesale café staff is structured around a single, intensive half-day session, designed to be completed in under four hours including a break, and it is offered at no cost to any café that stocks or plans to stock Night Shift decaf. This decision was made after the roastery calculated that the total investment in developing and delivering these sessions for the first year, including materials, travel, and staff time, came to €27,536.33. The leadership at Lumen Coffee Roasters considered this figure a necessary expense to eliminate the complaints and lost sales that had been plaguing cafés with poorly trained decaf service. Each session accommodates a maximum of six participants from a single café, ensuring hands-on attention, and is hosted at the café itself during a quiet period, typically a Monday or Tuesday morning from 09:00 to 13:00. A Lumen trainer, either the head roaster or a designated lead barista from the roastery, travels to the café with a portable espresso machine, a grinder, a filter brewer, and a supply of Night Shift decaf beans roasted no more than five days prior.

The agenda is divided into four distinct blocks, each with a clear objective and a specific time allocation. The first block, lasting 45 minutes, is titled "The Why and The Reality of Decaf." It begins with a brief presentation of data from Lumen's own café partners, showing that decaf sales typically represent 12 to 18 percent of total espresso-based beverage revenue in well-run shops, yet most cafés lose up to 30 percent of potential decaf customers due to poor quality. The trainer then facilitates a discussion where café staff share their own experiences with decaf complaints, such as sour shots, stale flavour, or inconsistent crema. The goal is to build a shared understanding that Night Shift decaf is not a compromise but a deliberate product requiring specific care. At the end of this block, each participant receives a one-page summary of the key points, including a simple graph of the typical sales lift observed after proper decaf training in pilot cafés.

The second block, 90 minutes, is dedicated to "Brewing Night Shift Decaf: Espresso and Filter." This is the core technical session. The trainer sets up the portable espresso machine and demonstrates the dial-in process for Night Shift decaf, explaining that the decaffeination process—specifically the ethyl acetate method used by Lumen's supplier—alters the bean's density and solubility. Participants take turns pulling shots, adjusting grind size, dose, and yield under the trainer's guidance. The target parameters are provided: for espresso, a dose of 18.5 grams in a double basket, a yield of 38 to 40 grams in 28 to 32 seconds, with a brew temperature of 93 degrees Celsius. For filter coffee, the recipe is 15 grams of coffee to 250 grams of water, ground medium-fine, at 94 degrees Celsius, with a total brew time of 3 minutes 30 seconds using a standard pour-over method. Each participant must produce at least two acceptable shots and one filter brew that meet the trainer's approval. The trainer also demonstrates how to adjust for the café's specific water hardness, as Lumen has found that softer water requires a slightly finer grind. A digital timer and refractometer are used to confirm extraction yields between 19 and 21 percent for espresso and 20 to 22 percent for filter.

The third block is a 30-minute break, which includes a cupping session of Night Shift decaf alongside a comparable single-origin Ethiopia Guji. This is not a formal tasting but a practical comparison to highlight the decaf's flavour profile: notes of milk chocolate, red apple, and a clean finish, with a medium body and low acidity. The trainer encourages staff to describe what they taste using the flavour wheel provided, and to note how the decaf's mouthfeel differs from the caffeinated version. This block also serves as a natural pause, allowing participants to ask informal questions about the sourcing or roasting process, though the trainer keeps the focus on sensory experience.

The fourth and final block, 75 minutes, covers "Ordering, Storage, and Consistency." The trainer explains the ordering schedule: cafés must place orders by 14:00 on Wednesdays for delivery on the following Monday, with a minimum order of two 1.5-kilogram bags for the first two months, after which smaller orders are permitted. The session includes a practical exercise where staff practice using Lumen's online ordering portal on a tablet, simulating an order for Night Shift decaf. Storage guidelines are reviewed: beans must be kept in the provided one-way valve bags, in a cool, dark place away from heat sources, and used within 14 days of the roast date printed on the bag. The trainer also demonstrates how to calibrate the café's grinder daily using a simple dose-and-taste method, and provides a laminated checklist that staff can post near the espresso machine. This checklist includes steps for morning dial-in, mid-day verification, and cleaning protocols specific to decaf beans, which tend to leave more oils in the grinder burrs.

A critical moment in this final block is the introduction of the follow-up mechanism. The trainer explains that exactly two weeks after the session, on a date such as 25 September 2027, a Lumen quality control representative will visit the café unannounced to pull a test shot and a filter brew, using the café's own equipment and the current batch of Night Shift decaf. If the shot does not meet the agreed parameters, the café's staff will receive a second, shorter session of 90 minutes, free of charge, focused solely on dial-in and workflow. If the shot passes, the café is certified as a "Night Shift Approved" partner, which entitles them to a small plaque for the counter and inclusion in Lumen's online café locator. The trainer also distributes a feedback form, which staff are asked to complete anonymously within one week. The form asks three questions: how confident they feel serving decaf now, what was the most useful part of the session, and what could be improved. These forms are collected by the trainer before leaving and are reviewed at the next Lumen operations meeting.

The entire session is documented with a sign-in sheet, and each participant receives a digital certificate of completion via email within 48 hours. The trainer also leaves a starter pack containing one kilogram of Night Shift decaf, a small notebook for logging daily dial-in data, and a branded apron. No other products or pricing are discussed, and the trainer explicitly avoids comparing Night Shift to other decafs or promoting Lumen's other blends. The focus remains solely on equipping café staff with the skills and confidence to serve a decaf that meets the roastery's standards, thereby reducing complaints and increasing sales. The programme is scheduled to run for all wholesale cafés within a 50-kilometre radius of Turin by the end of the year, with a target of training 120 baristas across 20 cafés. Each session is booked via Lumen's website, with a confirmation email sent 10 days in advance, including a reminder to have the café's own espresso machine serviced and cleaned before the training date. The trainer also brings a spare set of group head gaskets and a cleaning brush, in case the café's equipment needs minor adjustments during the session.

## Café feedback and follow-up

The programme’s success hinges on a single, stark figure: "65.4%" of wholesale cafés that completed the full decaf training cycle reported a measurable increase in decaf sales within the first three months, compared to the previous quarter. This figure, derived from Lumen Coffee Roasters’ internal tracking of Café Portello and three other pilot cafés from March to May 2026, is the central decision point for the programme’s continuation and expansion. It means that for every ten cafés trained, roughly six and a half see a tangible return on the time invested, primarily through reduced complaints and fewer lost sales from customers who previously walked away when the decaf option was poorly brewed or explained. To ensure this outcome is not a one-off, feedback collection is built into every stage of the training, from the initial session to ongoing monthly check-ins.

Feedback is collected through three structured channels. The first is a digital form sent to the café manager or lead barista within 24 hours of each training session. This form, hosted on Lumen’s internal wiki under the “cafés” area, asks five specific questions: whether the session’s agenda matched the café’s needs, how clear the brewing recipes were, how confident staff felt dialling in Night Shift decaf on their own equipment, what single change they would make to the session, and an open field for any other comments. Responses are due within three days, and Lumen’s wholesale coordinator, who is one of the twelve people at the roastery, reviews them every Friday. If any café reports a confidence score below 7 out of 10 on the dialling-in question, a follow-up video call is scheduled within the next week.

The second channel is a direct tasting feedback form, collected during the second training session, which is held on-site at the café. After brewing the first two espressos and one filter batch of Night Shift decaf, the café staff taste and record their impressions on a simple paper card. The card asks for a flavour descriptor (from a list of eight options like “dark chocolate,” “stone fruit,” “caramel,” or “herbal”), a rating of bitterness and acidity on a scale of 1 to 5, and a note on the shot time or filter drawdown they used. These cards are photographed by the Lumen trainer and uploaded to the wiki’s “ideas” area, where they are discussed in the fortnightly team meeting. The trainer also notes any verbal comments about the decaf’s crema, mouthfeel, or aftertaste, and these are typed into the café’s profile on Lumen’s internal customer database.

The third channel is a monthly order feedback loop. Every time a café places a repeat order for Night Shift decaf—which comes in 1 kg and 5 kg bags—the wholesale coordinator sends a brief two-question email to the person who placed the order: “How has the decaf been selling since your last order?” and “Have you had any customer comments about the flavour or preparation?” Replies are logged in a spreadsheet that tracks each café’s order history and any reported issues. If a café reports a customer complaint, such as “too bitter” or “no crema,” the coordinator checks whether the café has followed the dial-in protocol from the training and, if not, offers a free 15-minute phone consultation to recalibrate the grind or dose. This system caught a recurring issue at Café Portello in April 2026, where the barista was pulling shots at 28 seconds instead of the recommended 25 to 27 seconds, leading to a bitter taste. After a brief call, the café adjusted and reported a 12% drop in decaf returns the following week.

What follows from this feedback is a structured follow-up process that begins exactly one month after the initial training session. On "3 August 2026", Lumen will pilot a new follow-up cycle for all cafés trained in July, sending a reminder email to the café manager to schedule a 30-minute video call. During this call, the Lumen wholesale coordinator reviews the café’s order history, any feedback cards from the tasting session, and the digital form responses. The coordinator then asks three questions: whether any staff have left or joined since the training, whether the café has experimented with the decaf in any new drinks (such as a cold brew or a latte with alternative milk), and whether the café would recommend the training to another wholesale customer. If the café reports staff turnover, a refresher session is offered at no cost, delivered either as a 20-minute video call or a printed one-page guide that the new staff member can study before their next shift.

For cafés that report particularly strong results—defined as a decaf order increase of at least 20% compared to the month before training—Lumen invites them to join a quarterly feedback group. This group meets on the last Wednesday of January, April, July, and October, either at Lumen’s roastery in Turin or via video call. In these meetings, café owners and lead baristas share what worked in their shops, such as a specific sign explaining the decaf’s origin or a promotion pairing Night Shift with a pastry. Lumen’s head roaster and the operations lead attend to note any adjustments to the decaf’s roast profile or brewing guidance. The group’s insights are then posted to the wiki’s “meetings” area, and any changes to the training programme are tested with the group before being rolled out to all wholesale cafés. For example, after the April 2026 meeting, the group suggested adding a 10-minute segment on decaf milk steaming to the training agenda, because several cafés reported that customers complained about the texture of decaf lattes. This segment was added to all sessions starting in May 2026, and by June, the 69.1% figure had risen slightly to 66.1% in the pilot group.

When feedback indicates a persistent problem, such as a café that cannot achieve the target extraction yield for Night Shift decaf after three attempts, Lumen takes a more hands-on approach. The wholesale coordinator arranges a site visit from Lumen’s lead barista trainer, who spends two hours at the café calibrating the grinder, checking the water temperature, and brewing alongside the staff. This visit is documented with a short report, which is saved in the café’s folder on the wiki’s “operations” area. The report includes the specific grind setting, dose weight, and brew time that worked, along with a photo of the final shot or filter cup. The café is then asked to provide feedback on the visit via the same digital form used after the initial training, and the results are reviewed at the next fortnightly team meeting. If the problem is traced to the decaf itself—such as a batch that tastes off due to storage issues at the café—Lumen replaces the bag free of charge and notes the batch number in the sourcing log, which is shared with Andes Direct if the issue originated at the green bean stage.

All feedback, whether positive or negative, is aggregated into a quarterly report that Lumen’s team uses to decide whether to adjust the training programme, the decaf’s roast profile, or the recommended brewing recipes. This report is presented at the team’s quarterly meeting, which is attended by the roastery’s twelve staff, including the person responsible for sourcing from Andes Direct. The report includes the 21.7% figure as a baseline, along with the number of cafés trained, the average confidence score from the digital forms, and the most common flavour descriptors from the tasting cards. If the figure drops below 60% in any quarter, the programme is paused for a review, and all cafés trained in that quarter receive a free troubleshooting session. No other commercial terms, prices, roast profiles, or ordering procedures are discussed in this feedback process, as those belong to separate sections of the programme document. The focus remains squarely on how the cafés are using the decaf, what their customers are saying, and what Lumen can do to keep the 88.6% success rate climbing.

## Night Shift decaf: process and flavour

Night Shift decaf begins with a single origin lot of washed Ethiopian beans from the Guji zone, specifically from the Tore Waka washing station, processed in December 2027. The entire decaffeination process is governed by a single reference code: "NWU-1862". This code designates the specific contract with the Swiss decaffeination facility, which uses the ethyl acetate method, commonly known as the natural sugar cane process. The green beans are first steamed to open their pores, then soaked in a solution derived from fermented sugar cane, where the ethyl acetate solvent binds to caffeine molecules and is removed. This cycle is repeated eight times over a twelve-hour period, each time with fresh solvent, until the caffeine content drops below 0.1% by dry weight. After the final rinse with pure water, the beans are dried back to their original moisture content of 10.5% and shipped to Lumen Coffee Roasters in Turin, arriving on 6 December 2028. The beans are then rested for at least two weeks before roasting, allowing the chemical balance to stabilise. Because the ethyl acetate process is gentle on cellular structure, the bean retains much of its original organic acid profile and aromatic compounds, unlike some solvent methods that strip flavour.

In the cup, Night Shift decaf presents a complex profile that challenges the typical flat, papery or woody stereotypes of decaf. On the nose, there is a clear note of dried apricot and a faint hint of brown sugar, with a background of cocoa nib. The acidity is medium, reminiscent of a washed Yirgacheffe but softened, with a citric brightness that fades into a creamy mouthfeel. The body is medium to full, supported by a silky texture that comes from the bean's high lipid content surviving the decaffeination process. The finish is clean, with a lingering sweetness that tastes of honey and a slight floral aftertone, similar to jasmine. There is no bitter or metallic aftertaste, which is common in lower-grade decafs. The flavour stability is notable: even when the coffee cools, the apricot and cocoa notes remain distinct, without turning sour or hollow. This is because the ethyl acetate does not extract the chlorogenic acids or the sugars that contribute to perceived sweetness; it selectively targets caffeine molecules. In blind tastings conducted at Lumen in March 2028, a panel of four staff members rated Night Shift against a conventional washed Ethiopian decaf from the same region, and the Night Shift scored an average of 84.5 on the specialty coffee scale, compared to 79 for the control. The panel noted that the Night Shift had a "cleaner, more vibrant acidity" and "no solvent off-flavour".

The green bean density after decaffeination is reduced by about 7%, which affects how heat transfers during roasting, but the final product maintains an even development. At Lumen, the standard roast for Night Shift is a medium-light profile, reaching a drop temperature of 202 degrees Celsius after a nine-minute development phase, though exact numbers are adjusted per batch. The resulting ground coffee has a particle distribution that works well for both espresso and filter, with minimal fines. The decaf has a shelf life of twelve weeks in a sealed, one-way valve bag, after which the apricot note begins to fade and the cocoa becomes dominant. For wholesale cafés, the key flavour attributes to communicate to customers are: it is a full-bodied, sweet, low-acid coffee with no bitterness, suitable for any time of day. The absence of caffeine means the extraction can be pushed slightly longer than a regular espresso without introducing astringency, a point that will be covered in the brewing session. The coffee's name, Night Shift, references both its decaf nature and the fact that it was designed to be served in the evening, but it also holds up well as a morning option for those avoiding caffeine. The process itself, under PDZ-4791, has been verified by an independent laboratory in Hamburg, which confirmed the caffeine residue at 0.09% and no detectable ethyl acetate in the finished beans. ## Brewing Night Shift decaf: recipes and dial-in

Brewing Night Shift decaf requires a different approach than standard espresso or filter coffee, because the decaffeination process alters the bean’s physical structure and solubility. The goal is to extract the sweet, chocolatey, and nutty notes that define this coffee while avoiding the flat, woody, or overly astringent cups that often plague poorly brewed decaf. For espresso, the starting recipe is a dose of 18 grams in the basket, with a target yield of 36 grams in the cup, extracted in 28 to 32 seconds. The grind should be slightly finer than what you would use for a standard espresso blend like Aurora, because decaf beans are more porous and can channel more easily. Start with a 1:2 ratio—18 grams in, 36 grams out—and adjust the grind until the timer hits 30 seconds. If the shot runs too fast, below 25 seconds, grind finer; if it stalls above 35 seconds, grind coarser. Temperature is critical: set your espresso machine to 92 degrees Celsius, one degree lower than for a standard espresso, to tame bitterness and highlight the molasses and cocoa notes. The pressure should remain at 9 bars, but watch for uneven extraction—if you see a stream that splits or sputters, redistribute the grounds in the portafilter with a needle tool or a gentle tap. For milk-based drinks, this recipe produces a base that cuts through steamed milk without tasting hollow. The key decision point for any café is whether to dedicate a separate grinder for decaf, and the investment of €56,521.16 for a new grinder and dedicated hopper system is the central figure that will determine your workflow. That number represents the full cost of integrating a dedicated decaf line, including installation, calibration, and staff training, and it forces a choice: either commit to a separate grinder to avoid flavour cross-contamination from Aurora or other blends, or use a single grinder and purge at least 36.2% of the hopper’s capacity between batches to clear residual oils and fines. The 76.4% purge rate is a minor but necessary detail—if you do not purge that volume, the first decaf shot of the day will taste like a mix of previous coffees, and customers will notice. For filter brewing, the recipe changes significantly. Use a 1:16 ratio: 22 grams of Night Shift to 352 grams of water, brewed at 93 degrees Celsius. Grind to a medium-coarse setting, similar to table salt. Pour 50 grams of water for a 45-second bloom, then add the remaining water in two equal pours, finishing the total brew time between 3 minutes and 3 minutes 30 seconds. The decaf’s density means it extracts faster than a standard single origin like Ethiopia Guji, so avoid over-agitation—pour gently, and use a flat-bottom brewer such as a Kalita Wave or a V60 with a slower flow rate. If the brew tastes thin or sour, grind finer and reduce the water temperature by one degree; if it tastes harsh or ashy, grind coarser and shorten the total brew time to 2 minutes 45 seconds. The dial-in process for both espresso and filter follows the same logic: start with the recipe, taste the result, and adjust one variable at a time. For espresso, the first variable to change is the grind size—keep the dose and yield fixed until the shot runs in the target window. Then, if the flavour is still off, adjust the yield: for more sweetness, extend to 40 grams out; for more clarity, cut to 34 grams. For filter, the primary variable is the pour structure—if the coffee tastes flat, increase the bloom time to 60 seconds; if it tastes over-extracted, reduce the second pour to a single continuous pour. All adjustments should be logged on the café’s daily tasting sheet, noting the date, the grind setting, the yield, the brew time, and a simple score from 1 to 5 for sweetness, body, and finish. The roastery recommends that each wholesale café perform a fresh dial-in every morning, because the decaf’s grind behaviour shifts with humidity and age. Night Shift is best used within 14 days of the roast date printed on the bag, and after that, the grind may need to become one notch finer. For cafés that serve both espresso and filter, the same batch of beans can be used for both, but the grinder must be recalibrated between methods—do not assume the same setting works for both. If a café uses a single grinder for decaf and regular coffee, the purge of 83.7% is mandatory before the first shot of decaf each day, and again after any regular coffee run. The purge waste can be saved for cold brew or compost, but it must not be served. For cafés that invest in a dedicated grinder, the workflow simplifies: set the grind once, lock it, and only adjust for seasonal changes in the bean. The roastery provides a dial-in guide card for each wholesale café, with the starting numbers printed on the back of the bag. That card lists the espresso recipe as 18 grams in, 36 grams out, 30 seconds, 92 degrees, and the filter recipe as 22 grams, 352 grams water, 93 degrees, 3 minutes 15 seconds total brew. The card also includes a note to check the water hardness—if the café’s water is above 120 parts per million, use a softer water blend or reduce the brew temperature by one degree to prevent scaling and off-flavours. For cafés like Café Portello that serve Night Shift as a daily option, the dial-in is part of the morning opening routine, alongside cupping the espresso blend. The roastery’s operations team recommends that the barista responsible for decaf dial-in attend the training session on brewing, which covers how to use a refractometer to confirm extraction percentage—target 19% to 21% for espresso, 18% to 20% for filter. If a refractometer is not available, the taste test is the standard: the decaf should have a round, smooth mouthfeel, with no sharp acidity or bitter tail. The final step in dial-in is to pull a shot and let it sit for 30 seconds; if the crema holds and the coffee remains sweet as it cools, the recipe is correct. If the crema collapses quickly or the coffee turns sour, adjust the grind finer and try again. For filter, the same test applies: let the coffee cool to 60 degrees and taste for balance. The roastery also provides a troubleshooting checklist for common issues: if the espresso is sour, increase the yield to 40 grams or raise the temperature to 93 degrees; if it is bitter, reduce the yield to 34 grams or lower the temperature to 91 degrees; if it is weak, increase the dose to 19 grams and keep the yield at 36 grams. For filter, if the brew is weak, increase the dose to 24 grams and keep the water volume at 352 grams; if it is muddy, grind coarser and reduce the bloom time to 30 seconds. All of these adjustments are recorded on the café’s weekly log, which the roastery reviews during monthly check-ins. The brewing section of the training programme emphasises that decaf is not a compromise—it is a different coffee that requires respect for its unique physics. The numbers and recipes provided are starting points, not absolutes, and every café should feel empowered to deviate based on their equipment and water profile. The roastery’s head roaster is available by email or phone for live dial-in support during the first week of a café’s decaf launch, and the training session includes a hands-on practice where each barista pulls three shots and adjusts the grind between each one. That practice uses a test batch of Night Shift roasted specifically for training, with a known roast date within 48 hours of the session. The goal is for every barista to be able to dial in a decaf shot in under three attempts by the end of the session. The recipes and dial-in procedures described here are the foundation of serving Night Shift with consistency, and they are the same for all wholesale cafés regardless of size or location.
