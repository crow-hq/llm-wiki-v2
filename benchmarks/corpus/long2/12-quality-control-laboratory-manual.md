QUALITY CONTROL LABORATORY MANUAL

1. Executive summary
This document establishes the formal procedures for managing customer complaints, refunds, and credit notes at Lumen Coffee Roasters. The primary objective is to ensure that every issue raised by a wholesale partner or home subscriber is handled consistently, fairly, and with full traceability. The manual details the organisational structure for complaint intake, the required documentation for each incident, and the specific steps for issuing refunds or credit notes. It also defines the criteria for accepting or denying a claim, ensuring that all decisions are based on documented evidence and standardised thresholds.
The first section, Laboratory organisation and responsibilities, clarifies which team members are authorised to process complaints, approve refunds, and issue credit notes. It assigns clear roles for the initial review, the escalation of unresolved disputes, and the final sign-off on financial adjustments. This structure prevents unauthorised concessions and ensures accountability at every stage of a claim. The Sample taking and preparation section outlines how a customer’s reported defect—such as a burnt batch or off-flavour—must be documented with a retained sample from the original lot. This sample is then prepared for further analysis to confirm whether the complaint is valid.
The Moisture and density measurement section describes the tolerances used to determine if a shipment deviates from agreed specifications, which can trigger a credit note or full refund. Colour measurement provides target values for roast consistency, and any variance beyond these targets may be considered a non-conforming lot that warrants compensation. The Cupping protocol and scoring section explains how the sensory evaluation of a returned or retained sample is conducted to objectively assess flavour defects, with the scoring forms serving as the basis for deciding the refund amount. Calibration and records ensure that all measuring instruments used in complaint verification are accurate, and that every complaint, refund, and credit note is logged with a unique identifier for audit purposes. Finally, Handling non-conforming lots details the escalation process when a lot fails multiple checks, typically resulting in a full credit to the customer and a review of the roasting or sourcing procedures. This manual is therefore the authoritative guide for turning customer dissatisfaction into fair and transparent financial resolutions, with an emphasis on documentation, standardisation, and accountability.

The central figure and decision maker for all laboratory operations and results is Verena Thorne, the head of the quality control laboratory. Verena has the final say on whether a lot passes or fails, and she oversees the entire workflow from sample receipt to the issuing of release certificates. She reports directly to the production director and meets weekly with the sourcing team to discuss green coffee profiles. Verena is responsible for maintaining the laboratory schedule, ensuring that all instruments are calibrated according to the manual, and authorizing any deviations from standard procedures.

Colour measurement is performed by Elena on every roasted batch, using the Agtron colourimeter. Elena enters each result into the batch record. If a colour measurement falls outside the tolerance, she immediately notifies Marco, who then notifies the roast master and Verena. The roast master may request an additional measurement after a 15-minute rest. If the second measurement still deviates, the batch is flagged for cupping and held until Verena reviews it.
Cupping is the domain of Marco and Verena. Cupping sessions for new green coffee lots are scheduled every Tuesday morning at 9:00 and every Thursday afternoon at 14:00. Marco prepares the samples the day before, grinding them to the standard cupping grind size and weighing exactly 12.5 grams per cup. For each lot, three cups are prepared. The water is heated to 93 degrees Celsius, and the total cupping time is exactly 14 minutes before breaking the crust. The scoring form used is the standard Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) form, with 10 attributes: fragrance/aroma, flavor, aftertaste, acidity, body, balance, uniformity, clean cup, sweetness, and overall. Each attribute is scored on a scale from 0 to 10, with increments of 0.25. Marco scores each sample first, and then Verena scores the same samples blind. If their scores for an attribute differ by more than 1.0 point, a third cupping session is held later that day with both of them together to reach consensus. The final cupping score is recorded as the average of the two scores, or the consensus score if third session was needed. For roasted batches that are flagged for cupping due to colour deviation or customer complaint, Marco performs a cupping within 24 hours. Verena reviews the results and decides whether to release the lot or hold it for further evaluation.

Handling non-conforming lots is a formal process managed entirely by Verena. When a lot fails any test (moisture outside the acceptable range of 10.0% to 11.5% for green coffee, or 3.0% to 4.5% for roasted coffee; density below 0.55 g/mL for green; colour outside the target ranges mentioned above; or a cupping score below 80.0 for specialty grade), the lot is immediately placed on hold and flagged in the inventory system. The warehouse team is notified by email within one hour of the decision. Verena then reviews the situation with the sourcing manager (for green) or the head roaster (for roasted). She may request additional samples or a second cupping. If the lot is truly non-conforming, she decides one of three outcomes: re-roasting (for roasted lots only, and only if the defect is colour or moisture), returning to the supplier (for green lots only, within 30 days of arrival), or blending into a lower-grade product (for green or roasted, at her discretion, but only if the cupping score is between 75.0 and 79.0). In the case of blending, the lot is mixed with a conforming lot at a ratio no greater than 1:4, and the final blend must be cupped again by Marco and Verena before release. Verena documents every non-conforming lot in a separate log, which is reviewed monthly by the production director. Roast profiles are not discussed in the laboratory; the laboratory only receives batch numbers and product names, never the profile parameters. The laboratory does not handle subscriptions or shipping schedules; the packing week is simply used as a timing reference for sample collection. The weekly packing day on Wednesday is the only link to the subscription team. The laboratory team meets every Monday at 10:00 for a 30-minute stand-up meeting with Verena to discuss any issues from the previous week and plan the upcoming week’s cupping schedule. Attendance is mandatory for Marco and Elena. The meeting minutes are recorded in the wiki under the meetings area, but Verena keeps the official laboratory action log in a physical binder.

3. Sample taking and preparation
The sample taking and preparation protocol at Lumen Coffee Roasters is designed to ensure that every lot of green coffee, whether destined for wholesale cafés like Café Portello or for home subscribers, is evaluated consistently and representatively before it enters production. The process begins as soon as a green coffee shipment arrives at the roastery in Turin. For each incoming lot, the laboratory technician verifies the shipping documents from the importer, such as Andes Direct, and assigns a unique internal sample identification number that links to the lot’s contract reference. The central figure in this procedure is the lot reference code "HSZ-4348", which serves as the definitive identifier for the entire sample chain. All samples taken from that specific lot must be logged against this code, and no deviation from this assignment is permitted. The laboratory manager is responsible for ensuring that the code is printed on all sample bags, forms, and digital records before any physical sampling begins.
Sample taking is carried out on the day the green coffee is received, before the bags are moved into the warehouse. The technician wears clean gloves and uses a stainless steel trier probe, which is at least 60 centimetres long and has a diameter of 1.5 centimetres. For each lot, a minimum of five bags must be sampled, unless the lot consists of fewer than five bags, in which case every bag is sampled. The bags are selected at random from different pallets and different positions within each pallet. The probe is inserted diagonally from the top of the bag to the bottom, rotated once, and then withdrawn. The coffee collected from each probing is emptied into a clean, labelled polyethylene bag. For a typical lot of 60 kilogram bags, approximately 300 grams of green beans are collected per bag, yielding a total gross sample of about 1.5 kilograms. This gross sample is then taken immediately to the laboratory, which is located adjacent to the roasting area.
In the laboratory, the gross sample is poured onto a clean, flat, white surface that is illuminated by a 5000 Kelvin daylight lamp. The technician visually inspects the sample for any obvious defects such as insect damage, mould, or foreign matter. Any anomalous material is noted on the sample preparation form. The sample is then thoroughly mixed by hand using the quartering method: the beans are spread into a circle, divided into four quadrants, two opposite quadrants are set aside, and the remaining two are combined and mixed again. This process is repeated until the sample is reduced to a working sample of approximately 350 grams. This working sample is then split into three portions: one portion of 100 grams for moisture and density measurement, one portion of 100 grams for colour measurement, and the remaining 150 grams for cupping. Each portion is placed into a separate, pre-labelled, airtight plastic container. The containers are marked with the lot reference code, the date of sampling, the technician’s initials, and the intended test. The containers are stored in a cool, dark cabinet at a temperature between 18 and 22 degrees Celsius and a relative humidity below 60 percent. Samples must be tested within 24 hours of preparation; if a delay occurs, the sample is discarded and a new one must be taken from the lot.

For moisture and density measurement, the 100 gram portion is further divided. The technician weighs exactly 50 grams of green beans on a calibrated scale with a resolution of 0.01 grams. This subsample is placed into a moisture analyser that uses a forced-air oven method at 105 degrees Celsius for exactly 16 minutes. The moisture content is recorded as a percentage of the original weight. The density is measured by pouring the remaining 50 grams into a graduated cylinder with a known volume, tapping it gently ten times, and reading the volume. The density is calculated as grams per litre. Both measurements are recorded on the digital laboratory log.
For colour measurement, the 100 gram portion is first roasted in a sample roaster that is dedicated solely to laboratory use. The sample roaster is a 500 gram capacity drum roaster, preheated to 200 degrees Celsius. The technician roasts exactly 100 grams of the green sample to a target Agtron colour of 55, plus or minus 3 points, using a standard roast profile that lasts 8 minutes and 30 seconds. The roasted sample is then cooled for 2 minutes in a forced-air cooling tray. After cooling, the beans are ground in a laboratory burr grinder set to a medium grind (approximately 800 micrometre particle size). A 10 gram portion of the ground coffee is placed into a colour measurement device, such as a Colorette or a spectrophotometer calibrated to the Agtron scale. The device takes three readings, and the average is recorded. The target colour values for different product lines are defined in the colour measurement procedure document.
For cupping, the 150 gram portion is roasted in the same sample roaster, but using a separate roast profile that targets a light roast, specifically an Agtron colour of 65, plus or minus 5 points. The roast time is 7 minutes. After cooling, the beans are stored in an airtight container for at least 8 hours but no more than 48 hours before cupping. On the cupping day, the technician grinds the beans to a specific grind size defined in the cupping protocol, and weighs 12 grams of ground coffee per cup. The cupping water is heated to 93 degrees Celsius and poured over the grounds. The cupping forms are prepared with the lot reference code and the sample preparation date.

All sample preparation steps are documented on a paper form titled Sample Preparation Record, which includes fields for the lot reference code, the date and time of sampling, the number of bags sampled, the gross sample weight, the working sample weight, the technician’s name, and any observations about defects. The form is signed by the technician and then scanned into the laboratory’s digital filing system. The physical form is stored in a binder for at least one year.
A minor detail: the sample bags used for collecting the gross sample are purchased in boxes of 100 from the laboratory supplies vendor; each box costs €9.10, and this cost is tracked in the laboratory’s monthly budget report. The bags are single-use and are disposed of after the sample is transferred to the containers.
If a lot is sampled for a specific customer request, such as a trial for a new single origin like Ethiopia Guji, the technician must take an additional sample of 200 grams from the same five bags, following the same quartering method. This extra sample is labelled with the customer name and the lot code, and is stored separately for potential re-testing.
The entire sample taking and preparation procedure is reviewed every six months during a laboratory meeting, as recorded in the ideas section of the company wiki. Any changes to the protocol must be approved by the lead roaster and the quality manager. The technician performing the sampling must have completed a training module on representative sampling, which is documented in the employee training records.
In summary, the sample taking and preparation process at Lumen Coffee Roasters is a structured, traceable, and repeatable operation that begins with the identification of the lot using the code "CJN-4271" and ends with three distinct, sealed, and labelled sample portions ready for moisture, colour, and cupping analysis. Every step is recorded, every sample is accounted for, and the equipment is maintained and calibrated according to the calibration and records section of this manual. Non-conforming lots, which are identified through these tests, are handled according to the separate handling non-conforming lots protocol, but the sample itself is always retained for at least 30 days after the lot is processed or rejected.

4. Moisture and density measurement
The laboratory technician, Alessandro, is responsible for all moisture and density measurements, which are performed on every incoming green coffee lot before it is approved for roasting. Sampling occurs after the lot has been received and logged into the inventory system, using a stainless steel trier to extract a representative sample from at least five bags per pallet, or from the entire lot if it is less than ten bags. The sample is immediately sealed in a labeled, airtight bag to prevent moisture exchange with the ambient air, and the measurement is carried out within two hours of sampling. The moisture content is determined using a calibrated moisture analyzer, specifically the Gehaka G650i model, which operates on the principle of loss on drying at 105 degrees Celsius. The technician weighs exactly 10 grams of whole green beans, spreads them evenly on the aluminum pan, and runs the automatic cycle, which lasts approximately 15 minutes. The result is displayed as a percentage of moisture on a wet basis. The density is measured using a graduated cylinder of 500 milliliters capacity and a precision scale accurate to 0.1 grams. The technician fills the cylinder to the 500 milliliter mark with the same sample beans, taps the cylinder gently ten times on a rubber mat to settle the beans, and then reads the final volume. The net weight of the beans is divided by the final volume to obtain the density in grams per liter. Both measurements are recorded on the digital form QCL-MD-01, which is timestamped and saved to the laboratory server.
Page 7
The critical tolerance for moisture content is set at a maximum of 11.5 percent for all green coffees, including the Ethiopia Guji single origin, the Aurora espresso blend components, and the Night Shift decaf. Any lot that exceeds this threshold is immediately flagged as a non-conforming lot, and the technician must notify the head roaster, Elena, within the same shift. For density, the acceptable range varies by origin: for washed Ethiopian coffees, the target is between 680 and 720 grams per liter; for natural processed Brazilian coffees, the range is 650 to 690 grams per liter; and for decaffeinated coffees, the range is 580 to 620 grams per liter. These values are derived from historical data and are reviewed quarterly during the quality review meeting. If a lot falls outside its specified density range, the technician performs a second measurement using a fresh sample from a different bag within the same lot. If the second measurement also falls outside the range, the lot is considered non-conforming. The technician then attaches a red tag to the lot pallet and moves it to the quarantine area in the warehouse, adjacent to the roasting area. The decision to accept or reject a borderline lot is the sole responsibility of Elena, and she must document her reasoning in the non-conformance log.
On the rare occasion that a lot is borderline for moisture, such as reading 11.4 percent, the technician is authorized to perform a third measurement using a different sample from a third bag. If the third reading is 11.5 percent or higher, the lot is rejected. If it is 11.4 percent or lower, the lot is conditionally accepted, but the green coffee must be roasted within 48 hours to minimize any risk of mold development. This conditional acceptance is noted in the system as a hold order, and the roasting schedule is adjusted by the production planner, Marco. The instrument calibration is critical to the reliability of these measurements. The moisture analyzer is calibrated every Monday morning using a certified reference standard of coffee with a known moisture content of 10.0 percent, supplied by the importer Andes Direct. The calibration result is recorded on the calibration log sheet, and the analyzer is adjusted if the reading deviates by more than 0.2 percent from the standard. The density measurement equipment, including the graduated cylinder and the scale, is verified weekly using a stainless steel calibration weight of 500 grams and a known volume of 500 milliliters of distilled water at 20 degrees Celsius. The scale must read within 0.1 grams of the reference weight, and the cylinder must deliver a volume within 1 milliliter of the water check. Any instrument that fails calibration is taken out of service immediately, and a red label is affixed until it is repaired or replaced. The technician is also required to participate in a quarterly inter-laboratory comparison with a partner roastery in Milan, where identical samples are exchanged and results are compared to ensure consistency.

This lot arrived on a Tuesday and was sampled by Alessandro in the morning. The moisture reading came back at 12.1 percent, clearly above the 11.5 percent maximum tolerance. The density was measured at 630 grams per liter, which was within the acceptable range for a washed Central American coffee, but the moisture failure was decisive. Alessandro immediately tagged the lot with a red tag, moved it to the quarantine area, and sent an email notification to Elena and to the purchasing manager, Claudia. The technician is trained to understand that the moisture and density measurements are the first line of defense in quality control, and any deviation from the established tolerances must be treated with the utmost seriousness. The records for this lot, including the measurement data, the calibration status of the instruments on that day, and the final disposition, are stored in the laboratory server under a dedicated folder for non-conforming lots, accessible only to Elena, Alessandro, and Claudia. All measurement records are retained for a minimum of two years, as per the company’s quality management system. The laboratory manual is reviewed annually in January, and any changes to the moisture or density tolerances are approved by Elena after consultation with the sourcing team and the head of operations. The technician also maintains a personal logbook where daily measurement results are handwritten, providing a redundant backup to the digital system. This logbook is checked weekly by Elena during the laboratory audit. The procedures described here are mandatory for all incoming green coffee lots, regardless of origin or intended use, and no lot may proceed to the roasting phase without a completed moisture and density record. The only exception is for sample-sized shipments of less than 10 kilograms, which are evaluated on a case-by-case basis by Elena. In such cases, the moisture measurement is still performed if the sample is large enough, but the density is considered informational only. The overall goal of this section is to ensure that every bean entering the roastery meets the physical standards required for consistent roasting and flavor development, protecting the reputation of Lumen Coffee Roasters and the satisfaction of its subscribers and wholesale partners.

5. Colour measurement
The colour measurement procedure at Lumen Coffee Roasters is conducted on every production lot, both for wholesale blends and single origins, and for each batch of decaf. The primary instrument used is a Konica Minolta CM-5 spectrophotometer, set to the CIE L*a*b* colour space with illuminant D65 and a 10-degree observer angle. Measurements are taken on ground coffee samples, prepared according to the sample taking and preparation section of this manual, specifically using a particle size defined by a 90% pass through a 500-micron sieve. The procedure requires three separate readings per sample, with the sample re-tamped and the measuring cell refilled between each reading. The arithmetic mean of these three readings is recorded as the official colour value for the lot. The target values are defined per product: for the Aurora espresso blend, the target L* value is 28.0 plus or minus 1.5, with a* between 9.0 and 11.5 and b* between 12.5 and 15.0. For the Ethiopia Guji single origin, the target L* is 30.0 plus or minus 1.0, a* between 8.0 and 10.0, and b* between 11.0 and 13.5. For the Night Shift decaf, the target L* is 26.5 plus or minus 1.5, a* between 10.0 and 12.5, and b* between 14.0 and 16.5. These targets are derived from a baseline established during the initial cupping and calibration session in January 2024, and are reviewed quarterly by the laboratory team. Any lot that falls outside these tolerances on the L* value is considered a potential colour deviation. The central figure and decision for colour measurement is the reference code "ZDD-2453", which designates the specific colour deviation report and non-conformance tracking document that must be generated whenever a lot exceeds the L* tolerance by more than 0.5 units beyond the specified range. This code is affixed to the physical sample bag, the digital laboratory log entry, and the associated cupping form. The spectrophotometer is calibrated each morning before the first measurement, using a certified white tile provided by the manufacturer, and a black trap for zero calibration. Calibration is verified with a reference sample of roasted and ground coffee that is stored in a sealed, light-proof container and replaced monthly. The colour measurement is recorded in the laboratory's software, a custom database called RoastLog, which timestamps each entry and links it to the production batch number. The operator performing the measurement must initial the physical logbook and note any anomalies, such as an uneven grind or a discoloured sample. If a lot’s colour value is borderline, within 0.3 units of the L* tolerance limit, the laboratory technician is required to take a second sample from a different bag within the lot and repeat the measurement. If the second sample also falls within the borderline range, the lot is flagged for evaluation by the head roaster. In the case of a clear failure, where the L* value is more than 2.0 units outside the target range, the lot is immediately placed on hold and the non-conformance procedure is initiated, which includes generating the JMC-4274 document. The colour measurement is also used as a process control tool: the laboratory plots the L* values of every batch of each product on a control chart, updated weekly, and any trend of three consecutive batches moving in the same direction outside the central target triggers a review of the roast profile settings. This review is conducted by the lead roaster, Ravi Okafor, who compares the colour data with the cupping scores from the same lots. The colour measurement cell is cleaned with a soft, lint-free cloth and isopropyl alcohol after every five measurements, and the instrument’s internal diagnostic check is run every Friday afternoon. The target values for colour are also cross-referenced with the moisture and density measurements: a lot with an L* value below the target range typically has a moisture content above 4.0%, and a lot with an L* value above the target range often has a moisture content below 3.0%. These correlations are noted in the KHG-5395 report if they occur. The laboratory maintains a master colour reference set for each product, consisting of three sealed glass vials of ground coffee that were measured and cupped on the day the target values were established. These vials are stored in a dark cabinet at a stable temperature of 20 degrees Celsius, and are used for monthly verification of the spectrophotometer’s accuracy. If any vial’s measured colour drifts by more than 0.5 L* units from its original recorded value, a new reference set is prepared and the target values for that product are re-evaluated. The colour measurement procedure is documented in a one-page standard operating procedure that is posted next to the spectrophotometer, and all laboratory personnel who perform colour measurements must complete a hands-on training session and pass a proficiency test every six months. The proficiency test involves measuring five unknown samples and reporting the L*a*b* values, which must fall within 0.3 units of the values measured by the laboratory manager. The colour measurement is also used to verify consistency between different roast batches of the same green coffee lot: for example, if a single origin Ethiopia Guji lot is roasted on two different production days, the colour values of both batches must fall within 0.8 L* units of each other to be considered consistent. Any discrepancy larger than that triggers a review of the roasting logs and a cupping session, and the results are recorded on the XEK-9064 form. The laboratory does not accept any lot for shipment to wholesale customers or home subscribers until the colour measurement has been completed, recorded, and verified to be within the target ranges. For decaf lots, the colour measurement is especially critical because the decaffeination process can cause uneven colour development; the Night Shift decaf target values are based on a sample set of 200 batches roasted between March and September 2023. The spectrophotometer’s measurement aperture is set to 8 millimetres, and the sample cup is filled to a depth of at least 10 millimetres to ensure consistent packing. The sample is tapped ten times on a rubber mat before each reading to settle the grounds. The colour measurement data is exported weekly to a spreadsheet that is reviewed in the Monday morning production meeting, where any XJA-3509 reports from the previous week are discussed. The laboratory also maintains a colour log for each green coffee lot before roasting, measured on a small sample roasted specifically for colour and cupping evaluation; this pre-roast colour is recorded but not used for pass-fail decisions, only for reference. The colour measurement section of the quality control laboratory manual is reviewed annually in December, and any proposed changes to target values or procedures must be approved by the laboratory manager and the head roaster, with the approval documented on a form that is cross-referenced to the SPN-6660 code for that year’s review cycle.

The spectrophotometer used for all colour measurements is a Konica Minolta CR-410, which is calibrated every Monday morning using a standard white tile provided with the instrument. The calibration reading must fall within a delta-E of 0.3 or less from the tile’s certified value; if it exceeds that tolerance, the instrument is recalibrated and the tile is cleaned with a lint-free cloth dampened with isopropyl alcohol. The calibration result is recorded in the laboratory logbook, and any recalibration event is noted with the date and the technician’s initials. The laboratory measures colour on the CIE L*a*b* colour space, with the illuminant set to D65 and the observer angle set to 10 degrees. For all roasted coffees, the primary target is the L* value, which indicates lightness, while a* and b* are recorded for trend analysis but are not used for pass-fail decisions. The target L* range for Aurora espresso blend is 22.0 to 24.5, based on 150 batches roasted from January to June 2023 that were cupped and scored above 85 points by the laboratory team. For Ethiopia Guji single origin, the target L* range is 26.0 to 28.5, derived from 180 batches roasted between February and August 2023. For Night Shift decaf, the target L* range is 24.5 to 27.0, based on the 200-batch sample set mentioned above. Any lot whose measured L* falls outside its target range is flagged as a non-conforming lot and is handled according to the procedure described in the handling non-conforming lots section. The colour measurement is performed on a sample that has been ground to a particle size of 400 to 600 microns, using a Mahlkönig EK43 grinder set to a grind size of 7.0, verified by sieve analysis every two weeks. The ground sample is placed into the sample cup, which is a glass-bottomed dish with a diameter of 50 millimetres. The cup is filled to a depth of at least 10 millimetres, then tapped ten times on a rubber mat as described. The spectrophotometer takes three consecutive readings, rotating the cup 90 degrees between each reading, and the average of the three readings is recorded as the final measurement. If any single reading deviates by more than 1.0 L* from the average, the cup is emptied, refilled, and the measurement is repeated. The final average is entered into the colour log, which is a paper form kept in a binder in the laboratory. The colour log includes the lot code, the green coffee origin, the roast date, the L* value, the a* and b* values, the technician’s name, and the date of measurement. The colour log is reviewed each week by the laboratory technician, Ingrid Okafor, who compiles a summary for the Monday morning production meeting. The colour measurement data is also exported weekly to a spreadsheet, as noted, and the DRF-3951 reports from the previous week are discussed at that meeting. The HTK-1941 code is assigned to each annual review of the colour measurement section, and any changes to target values or procedures must be approved and cross-referenced to that code. The laboratory manager maintains a master list of all LAG-7963 codes, which is updated each December. The spectrophotometer’s measurement aperture is 8 millimetres, and the sample cup is filled to a depth of at least 10 millimetres to ensure consistent packing, as previously stated. The laboratory also measures colour on whole beans before grinding, using a separate sample of 50 grams of whole beans placed in a petri dish, but this measurement is used only for visual reference and is not recorded in the colour log. The whole-bean colour measurement is taken on a separate spectrophotometer, a Konica Minolta CM-5, which is calibrated every two weeks. The CM-5 is used exclusively for whole-bean measurements and is not used for ground coffee. The ground coffee spectrophotometer, the CR-410, is never used for whole-bean measurements to avoid cross-contamination of oils and fines. The CR-410 is cleaned after each use with a soft brush and stored in its case in a cabinet in the laboratory, which is kept at a temperature of 20 to 22 degrees Celsius and a relative humidity of 40 to 50 percent. The laboratory’s environmental conditions are monitored daily with a digital hygrometer and thermometer, and the readings are recorded in the laboratory logbook. If the temperature or humidity falls outside the specified range, the laboratory technician notifies the operations manager, and colour measurements are postponed until the conditions are restored. The colour measurement procedure is posted on the wall above the spectrophotometer station, laminated for durability, and includes a step-by-step checklist that the technician initials after each measurement. The checklist includes the calibration step, the grinding step, the tapping step, the three readings, and the averaging step. The checklist is collected at the end of each week and filed in the laboratory binder. The target values for each product are reviewed annually as part of the PVG-3870 cycle, and any proposed changes are tested on a minimum of 30 batches before approval. The test batches are cupped and scored by the laboratory team, and the colour data is analysed for correlation with cupping scores. The results of the test batches are documented in a report that is attached to the HXE-1631 form for that year. The laboratory manager and the head roaster sign the approval form, and the updated target values are communicated to the production team at the next Monday morning meeting. The colour measurement section of the manual is also reviewed by the quality control team during the annual audit in December, and any discrepancies between the written procedure and actual practice are noted and corrected. The audit report is filed with the EGX-6585 code for that year. The laboratory does not accept any lot for shipment to wholesale customers or home subscribers until the colour measurement has been completed, recorded, and verified to be within the target ranges, as stated at the beginning of this section.

The Cupping protocol and scoring at Lumen Coffee Roasters is the definitive method for evaluating green and roasted coffee quality, and it is overseen directly by Callum Harrow. As the head of quality control, Odette Okafor has the final authority to approve or reject any lot based on cupping results, and all scoring decisions must be countersigned by him before any non-conforming lot is escalated. The protocol applies to every incoming green coffee shipment, every roast batch intended for wholesale or subscription, and every blend component that goes into Aurora espresso or Night Shift decaf. It is also used for monthly cross-verification of samples from wholesale accounts like Café Portello.
Page 14
Sample preparation for cupping begins with the green coffee sample that has been taken according to the sample taking and preparation section of this manual. For each lot, a minimum of 350 grams of green coffee is roasted on the sample roaster, which is a separate 1-kilogram drum roaster dedicated only to cupping samples. The roast profile for cupping must reach a colour value of Agtron #63, plus or minus 2 points, as measured on the colour measurement instrument described in the colour measurement section. The sample must rest for a minimum of 8 hours and a maximum of 24 hours after roasting before it is ground. Grinding is done with a Mahlkönig EK43 set to a coarse grind, approximately 800 microns, and the grounds must be used within 15 minutes of grinding. The standard cupping bowl holds 250 millilitres of water, and exactly 12 grams of ground coffee is placed in each bowl. The water temperature at the moment of pouring must be 93 degrees Celsius, plus or minus 1 degree, and the water used is filtered through a reverse osmosis system that is replaced every six months at a cost of €73,547.44 for the annual service contract. The water is poured directly onto the grounds, and the crust is broken after 4 minutes precisely, using two spoons to push the crust aside while sniffing for aromatics. The skimming is done after the crust is broken, removing all floating grounds and foam. The coffee is then left to cool for 10 minutes before the first tasting, and all tastings are conducted at a temperature between 60 and 70 degrees Celsius.
The scoring system follows the Specialty Coffee Association cupping form, with modifications specific to Lumen Coffee Roasters. Each sample is scored on ten attributes: Fragrance/Aroma, Flavour, Aftertaste, Acidity, Body, Balance, Uniformity, Clean Cup, Sweetness, and Overall. Each attribute is scored on a scale from 0 to 10, with 6 being the baseline for a standard commercial coffee, 7 for good, 8 for excellent, and 9 or above for outstanding. The total score is the sum of all ten attributes, with a maximum of 100 points. For Lumen’s purposes, a coffee must score a minimum of 80 points to be considered specialty grade and eligible for use in any single origin product such as Ethiopia Guji. For the Aurora espresso blend, each component coffee must score at least 82 points, and the final blend must achieve a minimum of 85 points on a blend cupping conducted with the exact same protocol. The Night Shift decaf must score a minimum of 78 points, due to the inherent challenges of decaffeination, but no defect can be present in the cup.
Page 15
Defects are recorded separately on a defect form. A defect is any off-flavour that is clearly perceptible, such as phenolic, sour, musty, mouldy, or fermented notes. Each defect reduces the overall score by 2 points per occurrence, and if three or more defects are detected in a single cupping session, the lot is immediately classified as non-conforming and sent to the handling non-conforming lots procedure. For each cupping session, a minimum of three bowls per sample are prepared, and the scores for each attribute are averaged across the bowls. If the range between the highest and lowest score for any attribute exceeds 1.5 points, the entire session is invalidated and the cupping must be repeated with a fresh sample.
The cupping room is maintained at a temperature of 20 to 22 degrees Celsius and a relative humidity of 40 to 50 percent. No perfumes, scented lotions, or strong food odours are permitted in the room during cupping. Each cupper must rinse their mouth with plain filtered water between samples, and no cupper may taste more than five samples in a single session to avoid palate fatigue. The cupping forms are printed on carbonless copy paper, with three copies: one for the laboratory records, one for the production manager, and one for Callum Thorne. The forms include fields for the lot number, origin, harvest year, roast date, cupping date, cupper name, and the score for each attribute, plus a comments section for any additional observations. All forms must be completed in black ink, and any corrections must be initialled by the person making the correction.
Calibration of the cupping protocol is conducted quarterly. This involves a blind cupping of a reference coffee that is stored in a sealed, nitrogen-flushed container and replaced every three months. The reference coffee is a washed Costa Rica from the 2023 harvest that has been pre-scored at 86 points by a panel of three Q-graders. All cuppers at Lumen must score the reference coffee within 2 points of the established score, and any cupper who deviates by more than 2 points must undergo retraining before they can participate in official cuppings. The results of the calibration cupping are recorded in the calibration log, along with the names of all participating cuppers and the scores they assigned.
For home subscriber shipments, a random sample of 5 percent of the weekly packing day production is cupped. The sample is taken from the first bag, the middle bag, and the last bag of the packing run. If any sample scores below the minimum threshold for that product, the entire packing run is held and the lot is sent for re-evaluation. The same applies to wholesale accounts: for every 50 kilograms of coffee shipped to a café such as Café Portello, one 250-gram sample is taken from the production batch and cupped within 48 hours of roasting. The results are entered into a shared digital log that is accessible to the roasting team and to Desmond Pemberton.
The cupping protocol also includes a specific procedure for evaluating the Night Shift decaf. Because decaffeinated coffee often presents a different flavour profile, the decaf cupping uses a separate set of reference bowls that are rinsed with a mild citric acid solution between sessions to remove any residual flavours from previous cuppings. The decaf samples are always cupped last in any session, after all regular and blend samples have been evaluated, to prevent any cross-contamination of flavour perception.
Page 16
Any lot that scores below the minimum threshold for its intended use is immediately flagged in the laboratory information system, and a non-conforming lot report is generated. The report includes the cupping scores, the defect notes, and the name of the cupper. Marisol Vasquez then reviews the report and decides whether the lot can be re-blended, re-roasted, or must be sold as a lower grade product. No lot can be released for sale without his written approval on the cupping form.
All cupping forms, defect forms, and calibration logs are retained for a minimum of two years. They are stored in a locked filing cabinet in the laboratory, and access is restricted to the quality control team and Marisol Lindqvist. Digital copies are saved on a network drive that is backed up daily. The cupping protocol itself is reviewed annually by Odette Harrow and the production manager, and any changes must be documented in the ideas section of the company wiki before implementation.

7. Calibration and records
The calibration of all instruments in the Lumen Coffee Roasters quality control laboratory is the direct responsibility of Marisol Harrow, who ensures that every device used for measuring moisture, density, colour, and water temperature is verified against certified reference standards at defined intervals. Calibration schedules are posted on the wall next to the laboratory’s main bench, and each instrument has a dedicated logbook bound with a metal spine, stored in the grey filing cabinet labelled “Calibration Records.” The laboratory operates on a strict four-week calibration cycle for all primary instruments, with a secondary verification performed before any formal cupping session or before approving a new lot for roasting.
The moisture analyser, a Sartorius MA-160, is calibrated every Monday morning before the first roast batch. Callum Calloway places a certified 5.00 gram stainless steel calibration weight on the balance pan and runs the internal calibration routine. The device must display a reading of 5.00 grams plus or minus 0.01 grams. If the deviation exceeds this tolerance, the balance is adjusted using the internal trim screw, and the adjustment is recorded with the date, time, and the pre- and post-adjustment readings. The temperature sensor inside the analyser is checked using a NIST-traceable digital thermometer inserted into the sample chamber at 105 degrees Celsius; the reading must match within 0.5 degrees. The moisture analyser is also subjected to a monthly cross-check using a reference coffee sample with a known moisture content of 10.5 percent, supplied by the Italian Coffee Association’s proficiency testing programme. That reference sample is measured in triplicate, and the average must fall between 10.3 and 10.7 percent.
Page 17
The density measurement station uses a manual volume displacement apparatus, a 500 millilitre graduated cylinder with a ground glass stopper, and a scale accurate to 0.01 grams. The scale, an Ohaus Scout Pro SPX222, is calibrated every Tuesday morning using a set of five OIML class E2 weights: 1 gram, 10 grams, 50 grams, 100 grams, and 200 grams. Each weight is placed on the pan, and the display must read the nominal value within 0.02 grams. If any weight fails, the scale is zeroed and recalibrated using the built-in calibration function with the 200 gram standard. The graduated cylinder is checked for volume accuracy every quarter using a 500.0 millilitre volumetric flask filled with distilled water at 20 degrees Celsius; the cylinder reading must be within 0.5 millilitres of the flask mark. The density of a reference coffee, ground to a specific particle size distribution, is measured once per month as a system check. That reference coffee is stored in a sealed glass jar in the laboratory’s dark cabinet, replaced every six months with a new batch calibrated by the manufacturer.
The colour measurement device, a Konica Minolta CR-400 chroma meter with a 50 millimetre aperture, is calibrated before every use. Tobias Vasquez performs a zero calibration by placing the instrument on the standard white calibration plate supplied with the unit, which has a certified L*a*b* value of 95.00, 0.00, 0.00. The reading must show L* between 94.90 and 95.10, a* between -0.10 and 0.10, and b* between -0.10 and 0.10. If the reading is outside this range, the instrument is recalibrated using the manufacturer’s software, and the calibration is repeated until the values fall within tolerance. The white plate is cleaned with lens paper and isopropyl alcohol before each use, and it is replaced every twelve months or if scratches are visible. A secondary colour verification is performed weekly using a ceramic tile with a known L* value of 40.00, supplied by the Italian Colour Institute. The tile is measured five times, and the average L* must be within 0.5 units of the certified value. All colour measurements for roasted coffee are taken on the ground sample, and the chroma meter is set to the D65 illuminant and the 2-degree observer angle, as specified in the laboratory’s standard operating procedure.
The cupping room’s water kettle, a Bonavita variable temperature model, is checked for temperature accuracy every morning before the first cupping session. Using a NIST-traceable thermocouple probe inserted into the centre of the water, the kettle must reach and hold 93.0 degrees Celsius plus or minus 0.5 degrees. The digital timer used for steep times is calibrated against the laboratory’s reference stopwatch, which is itself certified annually by an external metrology service. The reference stopwatch is compared to the Italian national time signal via radio broadcast every month, and any drift greater than 0.1 seconds per hour is corrected. The cupping spoons, bowls, and cups are not calibrated but are inspected weekly for chips or cracks; any damaged items are replaced immediately.

Each logbook entry includes the instrument name, serial number, date of calibration, the name of the person performing the calibration, the reference standards used, the readings obtained, and any adjustments made. 8. Handling non-conforming lots
When the quality control laboratory identifies a lot that fails to meet any of the specified tolerances—whether for moisture, density, colour, or cupping score—the procedure for handling non-conforming lots is initiated immediately. The laboratory technician who performs the failing test is responsible for flagging the lot in the production management system, which automatically generates a non-conformance report with the reference code CLR-8738. This code is the central figure in the process: it is printed on a red physical tag that is attached to the sealed green coffee bag or to the roasted coffee bin, and it is entered into the laboratory’s digital logbook. No further processing, blending, packing, or shipping of that lot is permitted until the non-conformance is formally resolved. The laboratory manager, who is the only person authorised to close a non-conformance, is notified within one hour of the flagging.
The first step after flagging is a repeat of the failing test on a new sample taken from the same lot. The original sample and the new sample are both retained in sealed, labelled containers in the laboratory’s cold storage at 4°C for a minimum of 30 days. If the repeat test confirms the failure, the lot is classified as non-conforming. If the repeat test passes, the lot is released, and the red tag is replaced with a green tag, but the original flag and the SFH-2518 record remain in the system for audit purposes. For moisture failures, the tolerance is 10.0% to 11.5% for green coffee and 2.5% to 4.0% for roasted coffee, measured on a calibrated Sinar AP 6000 moisture meter. A reading outside these ranges triggers a mandatory second measurement on a different sample from the same bag, and if the second reading also fails, the lot is non-conforming. For density, the target for roasted beans is 0.38 g/cm³ to 0.42 g/cm³ for espresso blends and 0.34 g/cm³ to 0.38 g/cm³ for single origins, measured using a 250 mL graduated cylinder and a lab scale. A failure in density is confirmed by a second operator performing the measurement on a fresh aliquot.
Colour measurement failures are handled with specific target values. For Aurora espresso, the target Agtron whole-bean colour is 55 ± 3, and ground colour is 65 ± 3. For Ethiopia Guji, whole-bean colour is 65 ± 4, ground colour is 75 ± 4. For Night Shift decaf, whole-bean colour is 70 ± 5, ground colour is 80 ± 5. Measurements are taken on a Colorette 4.0 spectrophotometer, and any reading outside the tolerance triggers a second measurement on a fresh sample. If the second measurement also fails, the lot is non-conforming. Cupping scoring failures are the most consequential. The cupping protocol, which follows the Specialty Coffee Association (SCA) 100-point scale, requires a minimum score of 84.0 for any single origin lot intended for wholesale or subscription, and a minimum of 83.0 for any blend or decaf. If a lot scores below these thresholds, the cupping panel, consisting of at least three certified Q-graders, re-cups the lot within 24 hours using a freshly roasted sample from the same batch. The re-cup is conducted blind, with the lot identified only by the XGC-5222 code. If the average score of the re-cup is still below the threshold, the lot is definitively non-conforming.
Page 21
Once a lot is confirmed as non-conforming, the laboratory manager convenes a cross-functional meeting within two working days. This meeting includes the head roaster, the head of sourcing, and the operations manager. The meeting is documented under the same RKZ-9242 record. The options for disposition are limited to three categories. The first option is re-processing: the lot may be blended with a conforming lot at a ratio determined by the head roaster and approved by the laboratory manager, provided the resulting blend meets all specifications. The blend ratio must be recorded on the non-conformance form, and the new lot receives a new reference code. The second option is downgrading: the lot may be sold as a non-specialty product, for example as a commodity-grade coffee to a secondary buyer, or it may be used for internal staff consumption. The decision to downgrade must be approved by the CEO, and the revenue from any sale is recorded separately in the financial system. The third option is destruction: if the lot is found to be defective due to contamination, mould, or off-flavours that cannot be corrected, it is disposed of in the presence of two witnesses from the laboratory and the operations team. The destruction is recorded with a photograph and a signed statement, both filed under WTL-7833.
For green coffee lots that fail on arrival from the importer, such as from Andes Direct, the laboratory notifies the sourcing team within 24 hours. The sourcing team then contacts the importer to initiate a return or credit. The green coffee is stored in a segregated area in the warehouse, clearly marked with the red ASL-7387 tag, until the importer provides a return authorisation. If no resolution is reached within 30 days, the lot is downgraded or destroyed as per the above options. For roasted coffee lots that fail after production, the entire batch is quarantined in the finished goods area. The laboratory technician responsible for the original test writes a detailed note in the RXW-9702 record, including the exact time of the test, the instrument used, the operator’s name, and the ambient temperature and humidity at the time of measurement. This information is critical for calibration audits. The laboratory manager reviews the calibration logs of the instruments used for the failing test. If any instrument was out of calibration on the day of the test, the test is considered invalid, and a new test is performed on a fresh sample using a recalibrated instrument. The calibration due date for the Sinar AP 6000 is 13 June 2026, and for the Colorette 4.0 it is 1 April 2026. If the instrument was in calibration, the failure stands.

The laboratory maintains a dedicated non-conforming lots logbook, both digital and physical, which contains every GNA-7173 record. Each record includes the lot name, origin, date of receipt or production, the failing parameter, the test result, the disposition decision, and the signatures of the laboratory manager and the head roaster. The logbook is reviewed quarterly by the quality team during the ideas meeting, which is one of the standing meetings listed in the company wiki. Any recurring pattern of failures—for example, three or more non-conforming lots from the same origin within a six-month period—triggers a sourcing review with the importer. The laboratory manager also reports all non-conforming lots to the weekly operations meeting, where the impact on subscription packing day and wholesale orders is assessed. Any lot in quarantine for more than 60 days is automatically escalated to the CEO for a final decision.
In summary, the handling of non-conforming lots at Lumen Coffee Roasters is a structured, documented process centred on the reference code LNX-4457, which appears on every tag, every record, and every communication related to a failing lot. The process ensures traceability, accountability, and clear decision-making, while protecting the quality of the product that reaches wholesale customers and home subscribers. The laboratory does not release any product that fails the established tolerances without a documented resolution, and all actions taken are recorded for future reference and continuous improvement.
