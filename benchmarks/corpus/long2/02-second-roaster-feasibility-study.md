SECOND ROASTER FEASIBILITY STUDY

1. Introduction
This document presents a feasibility study for the acquisition of a second roaster for Lumen Coffee Roasters. The primary motivation is the current single machine’s capacity constraint, which limits production volume and restricts the ability to explore new products and roast profiles. The study evaluates all critical factors: technical specifications of candidate machines, physical installation requirements, financial implications, operational changes, and potential risks.
The analysis begins by detailing the existing roaster’s utilisation, identifying the specific bottlenecks in weekly production and how demand from wholesale clients and home subscribers is currently capped. From there, several candidate machines are compared based on batch size, drum design, energy efficiency, and control systems. The installation section examines where the second roaster would be placed in the existing facility, including necessary modifications for gas supply, electrical power, exhaust ventilation, and building works. Operating costs are projected, considering maintenance schedules, increased staffing needs, and the training required for a second operator.
Revenue scenarios are modelled around three levels of additional capacity, from conservative incremental growth to an aggressive expansion that would double current output. These scenarios inform the product ideas the second roaster would enable, such as a dedicated filter roast line, a decaf-only batch, or a light-roast single-origin series that requires a separate thermal profile. The study also addresses risks, including market demand fluctuation, machine downtime, and the alternative of toll roasting with a partner facility. Finally, the green coffee supply chain is assessed: how much additional raw coffee would be needed under each scenario, whether current importers like Andes Direct can meet the volume, and typical lead times for ordering and delivery. This comprehensive overview provides the foundation for a decision on whether to proceed with the investment.

2. Current roaster capacity and utilisation
The central figure in this section is the reference code "WNV-4833", which is the internal capacity audit Lumen completed on 14 February, covering the twelve months ending 31 January. That audit shows the single 15 kg Diedrich IR-15 roaster operated at 94.2% of theoretical maximum capacity, calculated on 208 working days per year, four roast days per week, and an average of 5.4 roast batches per day. The roaster is physically capable of 15 kg per batch, but Lumen’s average batch weight over that period was 13.8 kg, because green coffee density, drum loading limits for even development, and the need to leave headroom for safety margin reduced effective throughput. In practical terms, the roaster produced 41,760 kg of finished coffee in those twelve months, while the theoretical maximum at 15 kg per batch, 5.5 batches per day, and 208 days would be 45,760 kg. The gap of 4,000 kg is not idle time; it is lost to cooling time between batches, chaff removal, and the mandatory 20-minute rest period after each roast before the drum can be reloaded at safe temperatures.

The audit tracked every roast day from 3 January of the previous year to 31 January. Lumen roasts on Mondays, Tuesdays, Thursdays, and Fridays, with Wednesdays reserved for maintenance, cupping, and green coffee sampling. On a typical roast day, the roaster starts at 7:30 and finishes at 16:15, with a 45-minute lunch break for the two-person roasting team. The bottleneck is not the roast cycle itself, which averages 18 minutes for the Aurora espresso blend and 21 minutes for the Ethiopia Guji single origin, but the cooling and loading sequence. After each batch, the cooling tray runs for 4 minutes, then the drum must be swept and wiped down, which takes 6 minutes, and the next batch’s green coffee must be weighed, logged, and loaded, which takes 5 minutes. That 15-minute turnaround between batches, combined with the roast time, means the absolute fastest cycle is 33 minutes for Aurora and 36 minutes for Guji. The audit counted 1,123 total batches in the year, of which 612 were Aurora, 318 were Guji, 141 were the Night Shift decaf, and 52 were experimental or sample roasts for customer trials and new product development. The decaf roasts take 24 minutes per batch because the steam extraction process requires a slower ramp, which further compresses available time.
The utilisation figure of 94.2% is the headline number from VTW-8432, and it is already unsustainable. Lumen has no buffer for unexpected breakdowns, no room for a large wholesale order that arrives late in the week, and no capacity to roast a new product without displacing an existing one. In the last six months, the roaster has been stopped for unplanned maintenance on nine separate occasions: three times for a faulty gas valve solenoid, twice for a worn drum bearing, once for a cracked chaff collector door, and three times for thermocouple failures. Each stoppage averaged 2.7 hours, and because the roast days are fixed, those hours had to be recovered by either roasting longer on the same day, which pushed the team past 17:00, or by shifting production to the following day, which then overloaded that day’s schedule. The audit identified that Lumen had to decline or defer 14 wholesale orders between September and January, totalling 1,180 kg, because the roaster was already committed. Café Portello alone requested an additional 80 kg per month for its new breakfast menu, but Lumen could not guarantee delivery, so Café Portello reduced its standing order from 120 kg to 90 kg per month in November. Home subscription growth has been steady at 3.2% month over month, and the subscriber base now stands at 1,240 households, each receiving 1 kg per month, which consumes 1,240 kg per month, or 31% of total output. The subscription packing day is Thursday, and the roast schedule must prioritise Thursday morning roasts for Friday dispatch, which means the last two roast slots on Wednesday are effectively blocked, even though Wednesday is not a roast day, because the coffee must rest for at least 48 hours before packing. That constraint is not captured in the raw utilisation number, but it further reduces practical capacity by roughly 8%.

The roaster’s limits are visible in three specific areas. First, the batch size is fixed at 15 kg, and Lumen cannot split a large order into smaller batches without doubling the turnaround time. A single order for 300 kg of Aurora requires 22 batches, which at 33 minutes per cycle takes 12.1 hours, or more than a full roast day. In practice, Lumen splits such orders across two days, but that delays delivery and complicates the weekly packing schedule. Second, the roaster has a single drum, and switching between Aurora and Guji requires a full cleaning cycle of 40 minutes, including brushing the drum, vacuuming the chaff, and running a small batch of cheap beans to purge flavours. The audit counted 214 such switches in the year, consuming 142 hours of non-productive time. Third, the cooling tray capacity is 15 kg, but for the Guji single origin, which is roasted lighter and has a higher moisture content, the cooling time extends to 6 minutes, and the beans must be spread thinner to avoid uneven cooling, which reduces the effective batch size to 13 kg for that product. The audit shows that Guji batches averaged 12.9 kg, and the 318 Guji batches produced only 4,102 kg, whereas at 15 kg per batch the same number of batches would have produced 4,770 kg. That 668 kg shortfall is a direct consequence of the cooling limitation.
The roaster is also limited by its exhaust and afterburner system, which is sized for 15 kg and runs at 92% of its rated airflow during the peak of a roast. On days with high ambient humidity, which Turin experiences frequently in November and December, the afterburner must run at a higher temperature to maintain legal emissions compliance, which increases the cycle time by 2 to 3 minutes per batch. The audit recorded 11 days in December where the average batch time exceeded 24 minutes for Aurora, pushing the daily output below 80 kg. Lumen’s contract with the local utility, Torino Gas, allows a maximum draw of 120 kW, and the roaster uses 85 kW at full flame, leaving only 35 kW for the space heating and the hot water system used for cleaning. On cold mornings, the roaster cannot reach its target drum temperature until 8:15, 45 minutes after the scheduled start, because the space heater draws 20 kW, and the roaster must share the line. The audit notes that this cold-start delay occurred on 38 days in the year, totalling 28.5 lost hours.
The staffing model is also at its limit. The roasting team consists of two people: the head roaster, Marco, who has been with Lumen for six years, and one assistant, Elena, who was hired in March of the previous year. Marco works all four roast days, and Elena works three days, with Thursday off to handle the packing line, which requires two people for the subscription orders. The audit shows that Marco logged 1,876 hours of roasting time, which is 94% of his contractual 2,000 hours, leaving no time for cupping, green coffee evaluation, or equipment maintenance. Elena logged 1,408 hours, and her remaining time is spent on cleaning and data entry. The audit recommends that Lumen hire a third roaster operator before adding any new machine, because the current team cannot run two roasters simultaneously without sacrificing quality control. The report from PKZ-1846 explicitly states that the existing roaster is the binding constraint on revenue, and that any growth beyond the current 41,760 kg per year requires either a second machine or a fundamental change in operating hours, such as adding a fifth roast day, which the audit estimates would add only 12% capacity because the team would need rest days and the maintenance window would disappear.

The reference code "FBM-2457" appears in the audit as the maintenance log for the afterburner, which was serviced twice in the year, and the second service, on 19 December, revealed a cracked heat exchanger that reduced the afterburner’s efficiency by 18%. That repair cost 3,400 euros and took 11 days to source parts, during which Lumen had to reduce batch sizes to 12 kg to keep emissions within legal limits, losing an additional 380 kg of production in that period. The audit uses DEZ-1289 as an example of how the roaster’s age and wear are accelerating; the machine is eight years old, has roasted over 310,000 kg in its lifetime, and the manufacturer’s recommended service interval is every 500 hours, but Lumen has been stretching it to 700 hours because downtime is too costly. The audit concludes that the current roaster will require a major overhaul within 18 months, estimated at 18,000 euros, unless the workload is reduced. The utilisation figure of 94.2% is not a target; it is a warning. The roaster is the single point of failure for the entire business. If it goes down for more than three days, Lumen cannot fulfil subscription orders, and the wholesale accounts would need to be suspended, which would cause long-term reputational damage. The audit recommends that Lumen begin the second roaster feasibility study immediately, with a target decision date of 30 June, because the lead time for a new roaster is 16 to 20 weeks, and the current roaster’s condition suggests that a failure in the autumn peak season, when subscription orders typically rise by 20% for the winter months, would be catastrophic. The numbers from UZS-3496 are unambiguous: Lumen is operating at 94.2% utilisation, with zero spare capacity, a shrinking maintenance window, and a team that is already working at its maximum sustainable hours. The only question is not whether a second roaster is needed, but how quickly it can be installed and commissioned before the existing machine forces the issue.

3. Candidate roasters compared
Ravi Vasquez, Lumen’s head of operations, is the central figure driving this evaluation, and his comparative analysis of the three candidate machines forms the core of this section. The roasters considered are the Diedrich IR-12, the Probat P12/2, and the Giesen W15A, each representing a different design philosophy and capacity tier that must be weighed against Lumen’s current workflow and future needs.
The Diedrich IR-12 is a 12-kilogram drum roaster manufactured in Sandpoint, Idaho, USA, with a maximum batch capacity of 12 kilograms of green coffee and a typical production rate of 48 to 60 kilograms per hour when running back-to-back 12-minute roast cycles. Its drum is 400 millimeters in diameter and 600 millimeters long, constructed from 304 stainless steel with a 6-millimeter wall thickness. The IR-12 features a direct-fired gas burner rated at 60,000 BTU per hour, with an adjustable airflow system that includes a variable-speed exhaust fan and a cyclone collector for chaff removal. The machine includes a separate cooling tray with a 15-kilogram capacity, using a perforated stainless steel deck and a 0.5-horsepower fan to cool batches to room temperature in under four minutes. It has a manual gas valve for flame control and a touchscreen interface for recording roast data via Diedrich’s proprietary software, which logs time, temperature, and airflow per batch. The drum rotation speed is fixed at 40 revolutions per minute, and the roaster comes with a sample trier port and a bean-discharge chute that feeds directly into the cooling tray. The IR-12’s footprint is 1.5 meters wide by 1.8 meters deep by 2.2 meters high, requiring a dedicated 220-volt, 30-amp single-phase electrical connection and a gas supply line with a 3/4-inch NPT fitting. Its exhaust duct is 150 millimeters in diameter, and the manufacturer recommends a minimum clearance of 0.5 meters on all sides for maintenance access. The Diedrich is known for its thermal mass, with a drum that holds heat well during consecutive batches, making it suitable for consistent light roast profiles common in specialty coffee. However, its fixed drum speed limits the ability to adjust agitation for different bean densities, and the manual gas valve requires an experienced operator to fine-tune the energy input.
The Probat P12/2 is a 12-kilogram drum roaster built in Emmerich am Rhein, Germany, with a maximum batch capacity of 12 kilograms and a production rate of 50 to 65 kilograms per hour, depending on roast profile duration. Its drum is 420 millimeters in diameter and 550 millimeters long, made from cast iron with a 10-millimeter wall thickness, giving it very high thermal inertia. The P12/2 uses a gas burner rated at 70,000 BTU per hour, with a modulating gas valve that allows precise control of the flame intensity via a PID controller linked to a bean temperature probe. The airflow system includes a two-speed exhaust fan with a manual damper, and chaff is collected in a separate cyclone with a 50-liter bin. The cooling tray holds 14 kilograms and uses a 0.75-horsepower fan with a rotating rake arm to stir the beans, achieving cooling in three to four minutes. The roaster includes a digital display showing bean temperature, exhaust temperature, and roast time, and it can store up to 50 custom roast profiles. The drum speed is adjustable from 35 to 50 revolutions per minute via a variable-frequency drive, which is a significant advantage for handling different bean sizes and moisture contents. The P12/2’s footprint is 1.6 meters wide by 1.9 meters deep by 2.1 meters high, requiring a 230-volt, 32-amp single-phase electrical supply and a 1/2-inch gas line. Its exhaust duct is 160 millimeters in diameter, and the machine weighs approximately 850 kilograms, necessitating a reinforced floor if installed on a wooden surface. The Probat’s cast iron drum provides excellent heat retention, but it takes longer to preheat—roughly 45 minutes from a cold start—and the machine is more expensive to maintain due to the cost of replacement parts sourced from Germany. The P12/2 is a well-established model in the industry, known for its durability and repeatability, but its higher electrical draw and gas consumption may strain Lumen’s existing utilities.

The Giesen W15A is a 15-kilogram drum roaster from Ulft, Netherlands, with a maximum batch capacity of 15 kilograms and a production rate of 60 to 75 kilograms per hour, making it the largest candidate. Its drum is 450 millimeters in diameter and 650 millimeters long, constructed from a combination of stainless steel and copper, with a 5-millimeter wall thickness that provides moderate thermal mass with faster response times. The W15A uses a gas burner rated at 80,000 BTU per hour, with a fully modulating gas valve and a proportional-integral-derivative controller that adjusts the flame based on real-time bean temperature readings from a Type K thermocouple. The airflow system features a variable-speed exhaust fan with automatic damper control, and chaff is collected in a large cyclone with a 60-liter bin. The cooling tray holds 18 kilograms and uses a 1-horsepower fan with a dual-layer perforated deck and a rotating stirring arm, cooling batches in under three minutes. The roaster includes a touchscreen interface with Giesen’s RoastPro software, which allows for curve tracking, profile storage, and remote monitoring via a USB connection. The drum speed is adjustable from 30 to 55 revolutions per minute, and the machine has a built-in sample trier port and a bean-discharge system that uses a pneumatic actuator. The W15A’s footprint is 1.7 meters wide by 2.0 meters deep by 2.3 meters high, requiring a 400-volt, 16-amp three-phase electrical connection and a 3/4-inch gas line with a minimum pressure of 20 millibars. Its exhaust duct is 180 millimeters in diameter, and the machine weighs 950 kilograms. The Giesen is designed for high throughput and flexibility, with a faster preheat time of about 30 minutes and a more responsive drum that allows for aggressive profile adjustments. However, its three-phase power requirement is a constraint, as Lumen’s current roastery space is wired for single-phase only, and the larger exhaust duct may require modifications to the existing ventilation system. The W15A also has a higher initial cost and more complex electronics that could increase downtime if repairs are needed.

All three roasters are gas-fired, which aligns with Lumen’s current setup, but the Diedrich IR-12 and Probat P12/2 can run on single-phase power, while the Giesen W15A demands three-phase. The Diedrich is the most straightforward to install, with the smallest footprint and lowest utility requirements, but its fixed drum speed and manual gas valve limit its versatility for the varied roast styles Lumen may want to explore. The Probat offers better control with its adjustable drum speed and modulating gas valve, but its cast iron drum requires longer preheating and is less forgiving for rapid batch changes. The Giesen provides the highest capacity and most advanced control features, but its electrical and exhaust needs are the most demanding. Marisol Pemberton has noted that the Giesen W15A, with its 15-kilogram batch size, would allow Lumen to increase production by up to 50 percent compared to the current 10-kilogram roaster (a 10-kilogram model not detailed here), but only if the roastery’s infrastructure can be upgraded. The Diedrich IR-12 and Probat P12/2 offer more modest capacity increases but with lower installation barriers. A reference code, SNT-7891, appears on the Probat’s technical documentation as the model number for its gas modulation kit, which is a minor detail but indicative of the machine’s precision hardware. The choice among these machines hinges on Lumen’s willingness to invest in electrical upgrades and exhaust modifications versus accepting a slower capacity ramp. Each roaster also comes with a standard set of accessories: all include a cooling tray, a chaff cyclone, a digital control panel, and a sample trier, but the Giesen adds a pneumatic bean discharge and remote monitoring capability that the others lack. The Diedrich and Probat both have manual trier ports, while the Giesen’s trier is automated. The drum materials differ significantly: stainless steel in the Diedrich, cast iron in the Probat, and a stainless-copper composite in the Giesen, each affecting heat transfer and roast profile characteristics. The Diedrich’s drum is lighter and heats up faster, the Probat’s is heavier and more stable, and the Giesen’s composite offers a balance. The maximum batch sizes of 12 kilograms for the Diedrich and Probat versus 15 kilograms for the Giesen mean that the Giesen could produce up to 20 percent more coffee per hour when running continuous batches, assuming similar cycle times. The cooling tray capacities also vary, with the Giesen able to handle larger batches without bottlenecking. All three machines require a dedicated gas line with a shut-off valve and a pressure regulator, and each has a recommended minimum ceiling height of 2.5 meters for safe operation. The Diedrich and Probat are both available with lead times of 12 to 16 weeks from order to delivery, while the Giesen has a lead time of 20 to 24 weeks due to its custom electronics. Odette Vasquez has emphasized that the decision must account for not only the raw capacity but also the compatibility with Lumen’s existing roasting protocols, the skill level of the current staff, and the potential for future expansion. The Diedrich IR-12 is the most affordable and simplest to integrate, the Probat P12/2 offers proven reliability with moderate control, and the Giesen W15A provides the highest throughput and most modern features at the cost of significant infrastructure changes. Each candidate machine will be further evaluated in subsequent sections for installation feasibility, operating costs, and revenue potential, but this comparison establishes the technical parameters that will drive Lumen’s choice.

4. Installation, space and utilities
The existing roaster occupies the centre of the production floor at Lumen’s 220-square-metre facility in Turin’s Borgo Dora district, with the packing station to its east and green coffee storage to the west. The only viable location for a second roaster is against the northern wall, where a 4.5-metre-wide bay currently holds two pallets of empty valve bags and a seldom-used workbench. That bay, measuring 4.5 metres by 3.2 metres, provides 14.4 square metres of floor space, sufficient for a machine up to 2.8 metres wide and 2.4 metres deep, with clearance for loading, cooling tray access and operator movement. The workbench will be removed by 21 August 2028, and the bag pallets relocated to the mezzanine above the green coffee area, freeing the bay entirely. A 60-centimetre-deep concrete plinth will be poured, reinforced to bear 1,200 kilograms static load, with a 10-centimetre raised edge to contain any bean spillage and facilitate cleaning. The plinth will be finished with a food-grade epoxy coating matching the existing floor’s slip-resistant specification.
Gas supply is the primary utility constraint. The existing roaster draws 0.85 cubic metres per hour of natural gas at full burn, fed from a 32-millimetre pipe branched off the main 50-millimetre line that enters the building from the street. That line currently operates at 21 millibars and serves the roaster, a gas-fired hot water boiler and two space heaters in the packing area. A second roaster, assuming a comparable burner size of 120,000 British thermal units per hour, would add another 0.85 cubic metres per hour at full flame, bringing the total draw to 1.7 cubic metres per hour. The existing pipe diameter and pressure can sustain 2.1 cubic metres per hour without a drop below 18 millibars, so no upgrade to the street connection is needed. However, a new 22-millimetre branch must be run from the main line, turning east at the northern wall, with a dedicated shut-off valve and a manual reset high-temperature limit switch mounted within 1.5 metres of the roaster. The branch will be installed by a certified gas fitter from Turin’s Reti Gas service, who has already inspected the route and quoted the work at €2,150, including pressure testing and certification. A gas meter with pulse output will be added to the branch to allow separate monitoring of the second roaster’s consumption for cost allocation; this meter costs €480 and will be fitted during the same visit.

Power requirements are modest but specific. Each roaster’s drum motor, cooling fan and control panel draw a combined 2.8 kilowatts at 230 volts single-phase. The existing roaster is on a dedicated 16-ampere circuit breaker in the main distribution board, located in the office corridor 12 metres from the northern bay. A second 16-ampere circuit will be added to that board, with a new 2.5-millimetre-squared three-core armoured cable run in a steel conduit along the ceiling joists, dropping down the northern wall to a weatherproof IP66 socket near the plinth. The cable run is 14 metres, and the conduit will be painted to match the ceiling. The total electrical work, including the new breaker, cable, conduit, socket and labour by Lumen’s regular electrician (Elettrica Torinese), is quoted at €1,120. A 10-ampere uninterruptible power supply will be installed on a shelf above the control panel to protect the roaster’s programmable logic controller from voltage dips; that unit costs €320 and will be wired into the same circuit.
Exhaust and ventilation are the most complex installation component. The existing roaster vents through a 200-millimetre-diameter stainless steel duct that runs 6 metres horizontally to the exterior wall, then rises 4 metres to a roof-mounted exhaust fan and discharge stack. That duct cannot be shared due to back-pressure risk and fire code restrictions on combining two roaster exhausts. The second roaster will require a dedicated 200-millimetre duct, running 4.5 metres horizontally from the northern bay to the same exterior wall, then turning upward and joining a separate 5-metre vertical stack fitted with its own roof-mounted fan. The fan must be explosion-proof, rated for continuous operation at 250 degrees Celsius, with a capacity of 1,200 cubic metres per hour. The duct will be fabricated from 1.5-millimetre-thick 304-grade stainless steel, with welded seams and a 30-degree sloped section at the wall penetration to prevent condensation pooling. A manual damper will be installed at the roaster connection for balancing. The wall penetration requires a 250-millimetre core drill through 35 centimetres of brick and plaster, with a stainless steel sleeve and firestop sealant. The roof penetration through the clay tile roofing will be flashed with a lead apron and sealed with high-temperature silicone. The entire exhaust system, including the fan, duct sections, dampers, wall and roof penetrations, labour and scaffolding hire for roof access, is quoted at €9,780. A separate 150-millimetre fresh air intake will be cut into the northern wall, fitted with a motorised louvre that opens when the roaster is running, to supply combustion air without pressurising the room; this intake costs €640 installed.

Additional works include a compressed air line for the roaster’s pneumatic bean sampler and cleaning blow-off. A 10-millimetre nylon tube will be run from the existing 200-litre compressor in the packing area, which has adequate capacity at 8 bar and 350 litres per minute, but a new moisture trap and regulator will be added at the roaster location. The tube run is 18 metres, clipped to the ceiling conduit, and the regulator assembly costs €180. A fire suppression system is mandatory under Turin’s municipal fire code for any roasting appliance exceeding 15 kilograms capacity. The second roaster, even if smaller than the existing one, triggers the requirement because it is a new heat source. A wet-chemical system with a 9-litre tank, two nozzles over the drum and one over the cooling tray, and a manual pull station near the exit, will be installed by Antincendio Piemonte. The system includes a gas shut-off valve that closes automatically on activation. The quote is €3,450, with annual inspection included for the first year. The total cost for all installation, space and utility works, including gas branch and meter, electrical circuit and UPS, exhaust system, fresh air intake, compressed air line, fire suppression, plinth construction, relocation of the workbench and bag pallets, and all labour and materials, comes to €49,391.92. This figure is the central decision point for the feasibility study, as it represents the fixed capital outlay beyond the roaster purchase price itself. Lumen’s finance team has confirmed that this amount can be drawn from the equipment reserve fund without requiring a loan, provided the roaster purchase is delayed to the next fiscal quarter. The works will be sequenced over three weeks: week one for plinth curing and electrical rough-in, week two for gas and exhaust installation, week three for fire suppression and final connections. All contractors have been briefed and can begin within 30 days of a signed order. No structural modifications to the building’s load-bearing walls are required, and the existing fire egress path along the northern wall remains unobstructed after the plinth is installed. The new roaster’s operator station will face south, allowing visual line-of-sight to the existing roaster and the packing station, which improves supervision without increasing staffing. Utility consumption monitoring will be integrated into Lumen’s existing Building Management System via Modbus interfaces on the gas meter and the exhaust fan controller, enabling real-time tracking of energy use per roast batch.

The central figure in this section is Marisol Delacroix, who will be the lead operator for the second roaster. She currently runs the existing 15 kg machine three days a week and manages the green bean inventory on the fourth. With the second roaster, her role will shift to overseeing both machines, with her primary station at the new unit. She will train and supervise a dedicated assistant, a position currently budgeted as a part-time hire at 20 hours per week, starting at EUR 14.50 per hour. Callum Lindqvist’s annual salary is EUR 38,000, and the new assistant will cost approximately EUR 15,080 per year including employer social contributions. The total direct staffing cost for the second roaster is therefore EUR 53,080 annually. The owner, who is not Callum Harrow, will continue to handle all procurement and customer relationships, so no additional management overhead is allocated here. Running costs for the second roaster fall into three categories: energy, consumables, and waste disposal. The machine is a gas-fired model, and based on the existing roaster’s consumption of 0.35 cubic meters of natural gas per kilogram of roasted coffee, the new unit will use approximately 3,500 cubic meters per year at a projected output of 10,000 kg roasted annually. The local gas tariff in Turin is EUR 0.95 per cubic meter as of the current contract, which expires in December 2025, so gas costs will be EUR 3,325 per year. Electricity for the drum motor, cooling fan, and control panel adds another EUR 420 annually based on a 5.5 kW peak draw and a rate of EUR 0.18 per kWh. Water for the afterburner cooling loop is negligible, estimated at EUR 80 per year. Consumables include replacement thermocouples, which cost EUR 45 each and need replacement every 600 roasts; at 500 roasts per year, that is two replacements at EUR 90. The cooling tray air filter costs EUR 12 and is swapped quarterly, adding EUR 48. Lubricant for the drum bearing is a specialty high-temperature grease at EUR 35 per tube, used twice a year for EUR 70. Total consumables: EUR 208. Waste disposal covers chaff and silverskin, which are collected by a local composting service at EUR 25 per bin pickup; the second roaster will generate about 80 kg of chaff per month, requiring one pickup every two months, or six pickups per year at EUR 150. The total annual running cost for the second roaster, excluding staffing, is EUR 4,183.
Maintenance is planned around a preventive schedule, not reactive repairs. The manufacturer recommends a full service after every 500 roasting hours, which for Lumen’s planned 10-hour roasting days four days a week translates to roughly 2,000 hours per year, so four services annually. Each service costs EUR 320 from the local agent, covering bearing check, belt tension, burner nozzle cleaning, and calibration of the exhaust temperature probe. That is EUR 1,280 per year. In addition, the afterburner requires an annual inspection and carbon filter replacement at EUR 450. The drum door gasket, a wear item, costs EUR 60 and should be replaced every 18 months, prorated to EUR 40 per year. The cooling tray fan motor has a five-year warranty, but after that a replacement motor is EUR 280; setting aside EUR 56 per year as a reserve is prudent. Total maintenance reserve, including the warranty period, is EUR 1,826 annually. After the warranty expires in year three, the reserve should increase by EUR 200 per year for unexpected breakdowns, but for the feasibility study the first three years are costed at EUR 1,826. The combined operating cost, staffing, and maintenance for the second roaster in year one is EUR 59,089.

If demand spikes, a second part-time assistant could be added at 15 hours per week, costing an additional EUR 11,310 per year, but this is not included in the base scenario.

8. Risks and alternatives to buying
The decision to buy a second roaster ultimately hinges on one number: 67.9%. That is the utilisation rate of the existing 15 kg machine across the four roasting days per week, calculated over the last six months of production records. At that level, the roaster is not yet at a hard capacity wall, but it is firmly in the zone where scheduling flexibility has evaporated. Any unplanned downtime, a large wholesale order, or a new subscription cohort pushes roasting into evenings or forces Lumen to skip a planned maintenance window. The risk is not that the roaster breaks tomorrow, but that Lumen cannot absorb growth without degrading quality or delivery reliability. Buying a second machine is a bet that demand will continue to climb; the alternative is to accept that 27.7% utilisation is a ceiling, not a floor, and to manage the business within that constraint.
Page 18
The most obvious risk is that the second roaster sits idle. If wholesale accounts do not expand as projected, or if home subscription growth plateaus, Lumen would carry a machine that consumes floor space, requires insurance, and demands periodic testing and cleaning, all without generating revenue. The financial exposure is not just the purchase price, but the opportunity cost of capital that could have gone toward green coffee inventory, a second packing line, or a part-time sales hire. A related risk is that the second roaster is too small or too large. If Lumen buys a 30 kg machine, it might be oversized for the next two years, leading to inefficient half-batches. If it buys a 5 kg sample roaster, it solves the wrong problem, because the bottleneck is production, not experimentation. The candidate list must therefore be matched to realistic volume forecasts, not aspirational ones.
A second, subtler risk is operational complexity. Lumen currently runs one roast schedule, one maintenance routine, and one set of gas and exhaust connections. Adding a second machine means two warm-up and cool-down cycles, two cleaning schedules, and a more complicated daily roster for the three roasters on staff. The twelve-person team is small; if the second roaster requires a dedicated operator for even two days a week, that pulls someone away from packing, customer service, or green coffee receiving. There is also the risk of inconsistent output between machines. Even identical models from the same manufacturer can behave differently in terms of heat retention and drum speed, which means Lumen would need to recalibrate roast profiles for every blend and single origin on the new machine. That is a two-to-three-month project, and during that period, any batch roasted on the new machine that fails to match the old machine’s cup character could annoy wholesale clients like Café Portello, who have built their menu around a consistent Aurora espresso.
The physical installation itself carries risks. The roastery is in a historic Turin building, and the proposed location for the second machine is against the north wall, near the current exhaust outlet. That wall is load-bearing, and the gas line running to it is undersized. The utility survey from March showed that the existing 1.5-inch gas pipe can supply both machines only if the second roaster is connected after the main pressure regulator, but that would reduce pressure to the first roaster during simultaneous firing. The alternative is a new gas line from the street, which requires a permit from the city and a two-week wait for an inspection. The electrical panel has space for one more 380-volt circuit, but the exhaust fan would need to be upgraded from 1.5 kW to 2.2 kW to handle the combined airflow. None of these are deal-breakers, but they add cost and timeline risk. A realistic estimate is that installation and commissioning take six to eight weeks, not the four weeks quoted by the equipment supplier.
Page 19
The staffing risk is tied to the fact that Lumen has no dedicated maintenance person. The head roaster currently handles all belt replacements, burner nozzle cleaning, and drum bearing checks. Adding a second machine doubles that workload. If the head roaster is sick or on holiday, the second machine likely sits unused, because the other two roasters on staff are trained only on the existing 15 kg model. Cross-training is a prerequisite, not an afterthought. A more existential risk is that the second roaster accelerates the need for a larger facility. If the new machine enables Lumen to take on three new wholesale accounts and grow subscriptions by 40%, the packing area, which is already tight, will become the next bottleneck. The second roaster might be a solution to one constraint that merely shifts the problem downstream.
Alternatives to buying exist, and toll roasting is the most viable. Lumen could contract with a larger roastery in Milan that has idle capacity on a 60 kg machine. That facility, which Lumen has used once for an overflow batch of Ethiopia Guji in November, can roast to Lumen’s specified profile and ship directly to wholesale customers. The advantage is zero capital outlay and no installation risk. The disadvantages are loss of control over batch timing, a minimum order quantity that might force Lumen to roast more inventory than needed, and the fact that toll-roasted coffee cannot carry the Lumen name on the bag if the contract roaster refuses a co-packing agreement. For the decaf Night Shift, which sells in lower volume, toll roasting is attractive because it frees the existing roaster for higher-margin single origins. But for Aurora espresso, which is the core wholesale product, toll roasting would be risky because any variation in the roast curve would be blamed on Lumen, not the contractor.
Another alternative is to delay the purchase by six months and instead raise the price of subscriptions by a small margin, which would reduce demand enough to lower utilisation to roughly 62%, buying time without spending capital. That is a defensive move, and it does not address the long-term opportunity. A third alternative is to buy only a cooling tray and a larger batch hopper for the existing roaster, which would allow Lumen to push the current machine to 75% utilisation by reducing cycle time between batches. That is a low-cost, low-risk intervention, but it does not create any new product capability. A fourth option is to lease a second roaster on a three-year term with a purchase option. Leasing shifts the risk of obsolescence to the supplier, but the monthly payment is a fixed cost that Lumen must cover even in slow months. Toll roasting remains the most flexible alternative, because Lumen can scale it up or down with no commitment. The reference code for the toll roasting trial contract from that November overflow was AKA-3792, and that experience showed that the Milan facility could match the Guji profile within a 2% colourimeter variance, which is acceptable for a single origin but not for a blend.
Page 20
The green coffee risk is tied to the second roaster’s capacity. If Lumen buys a 15 kg machine identical to the current one, total capacity doubles to 120 kg per roasting day, assuming four days per week and two batches per hour. That would require roughly 60 additional kilograms of green coffee per week, or about 3,100 kilograms per year. The current importer, Andes Direct, has a lead time of 10 to 12 weeks for Ethiopian lots and 8 weeks for Colombian. They have confirmed that they can allocate an additional 3,500 kilograms per year from the next harvest, but only if Lumen signs a contract by the end of the current quarter. If Lumen waits until after the harvest, the price will likely rise by 8% to 12% due to scarcity. The bigger risk is not total volume but lot consistency. A second roaster would allow Lumen to run two different origins simultaneously, which means needing two distinct green lots in stock at all times. The current warehouse space holds about 1,800 kilograms of green coffee; adding a second roaster without expanding storage would mean more frequent, smaller deliveries, which increases freight cost per kilogram and the risk of running out of a specific lot between shipments.
The final risk is reputational. If Lumen buys the second roaster, promotes new products, and then fails to deliver because of installation delays or operator error, the trust built with Café Portello and the home subscribers is damaged. That trust is more valuable than any machine. The safest path is to treat the 63.9% utilisation figure as a trigger for a deeper review, not an automatic green light. Lumen should first secure green coffee contracts, then cross-train two staff members on a rented machine at the Milan facility, and only then commit to a purchase. Toll roasting is not a permanent solution, but it is a low-regret bridge. If the revenue scenarios in the next section show a clear path to 85% utilisation on two machines within eighteen months, the purchase is justified. If not, the better decision is to keep the single roaster, raise prices, and wait for the market to force the issue.

10. Importer capacity and lead times
Lumen’s current green coffee purchasing is handled through three main importers: Andes Direct, which supplies the bulk of the single-origin lots such as the Ethiopia Guji; a smaller Italian importer called Caffè Trieste for the Aurora espresso blend components; and a European decaf specialist, DecafCo, for the Night Shift decaf. The existing 15-kilogram roaster runs four days per week, averaging roughly 180 kilograms per day, or 720 kilograms per week, which is approximately 37,440 kilograms per year. A second roaster of similar size, running the same schedule, would double that capacity, though realistic utilisation in the first year is likely 60 to 70 percent, meaning an additional 22,500 to 26,200 kilograms annually. This volume must be sourced entirely through green coffee purchases, as no significant inventory buffer exists beyond a two-week supply in Lumen’s Turin warehouse.

Andes Direct, based in Lima with a European distribution hub in Rotterdam, currently supplies about 60 percent of Lumen’s green coffee by weight, primarily Ethiopian and Colombian microlots. Their capacity to increase supply is strong: they have confirmed in writing that their 2027 contract volumes for Lumen can be raised by up to 50 percent without lead time extension, provided orders are placed by 3 June 2027. This date is the key fact of this section and the central decision point for the second roaster feasibility study, because it marks the cutoff for Andes Direct to guarantee additional allocation from their 2027 harvest lots. After that date, any extra volume would come from spot market purchases at higher risk of price volatility and inconsistent quality. Andes Direct’s typical lead time for standard orders is six to eight weeks from order confirmation to delivery in Turin, but for the increased volume they would require a four-month lead for pre-shipment samples and container booking, meaning any order placed after 26 October 2028 would arrive no earlier than October 2027, which would delay the second roaster’s green coffee supply until the fourth quarter. Their minimum order increment for new volume is a single pallet of 720 kilograms, and they can supply up to six additional pallets per quarter, or about 17,280 kilograms per year, which covers the lower end of the projected extra need.
Caffè Trieste, which provides the Brazil and Guatemala base components for Aurora espresso, currently ships about 15 percent of Lumen’s total green coffee. They operate out of Trieste’s coffee port and have a more constrained supply chain: their annual contract with Lumen is fixed at 5,000 kilograms, and they have indicated they cannot increase this beyond 6,500 kilograms per year without renegotiating their own grower relationships, which would take at least nine months. For a second roaster requiring perhaps 3,000 to 4,000 additional kilograms of espresso blend base, Caffè Trieste could only contribute about 1,500 kilograms of that within the first year, and only if Lumen commits by the end of June 2027. Their lead time is shorter, typically four weeks from order, because they hold regional stock in Turin, but they cannot absorb sudden spikes. For the remaining espresso blend volume, Lumen would need to either source from Andes Direct or find a new importer, which introduces quality consistency risk for the Aurora blend profile.

DecafCo, based in Hamburg, supplies the Night Shift decaf, which accounts for roughly 25 percent of Lumen’s current green coffee purchases, or about 9,360 kilograms per year. DecafCo’s decaffeination process is booked a year in advance for Swiss Water Process lots, and they have already allocated Lumen’s 2027 volume at 10,000 kilograms. For the second roaster, Lumen would likely want to increase decaf production by at least 3,000 to 5,000 kilograms per year to serve new wholesale accounts and home subscribers who prefer a late-day option. DecafCo can supply an additional 4,000 kilograms, but only from their 2028 production cycle, which would not be available until February 2028. They charge a premium for rush orders, and one such premium is a flat €39.32 per kilogram for any decaf volume ordered outside the annual booking window, which would make a mid-year increase uneconomical. Therefore, any decaf volume expansion must be planned for the 2028 calendar year, with a commitment letter due by 1 September 2027.
Beyond the three main importers, Lumen occasionally buys from two smaller brokers: Green Bean Italia in Milan and a German trader, Rohkaffee GmbH, which together supply about 5 percent of volume, mostly experimental lots. These brokers have flexible but limited capacity: Green Bean Italia can supply up to 2,000 additional kilograms per year from their warehouse in Milan with a two-week lead time, but their stock is often seasonal and not always traceable to single farms. Rohkaffee GmbH can provide up to 1,500 kilograms of certified organic coffee per quarter, but their prices are volatile and they require a 50 percent deposit on order. Neither is a reliable primary source for the sustained volume a second roaster would require.
The total additional green coffee need for the second roaster, assuming 60 percent utilisation in year one, is approximately 22,500 kilograms. Andes Direct can cover 17,280 kilograms if ordered by 14 March 2028. Caffè Trieste can contribute 1,500 kilograms, and DecafCo can contribute 4,000 kilograms but only for 2028. This leaves a shortfall of roughly 3,720 kilograms for the first year, which could be covered by Green Bean Italia and Rohkaffee GmbH combined, but only if Lumen accepts the higher risk of inconsistent quality and price. Alternatively, Lumen could reduce the second roaster’s utilisation to 50 percent in year one, lowering the need to 18,720 kilograms, which would be fully covered by Andes Direct alone if the order is placed by 23 November 2027.
Lead times are a critical constraint. For Andes Direct, any order placed after 25 July 2026 will not arrive until late October 2027 at the earliest, meaning the second roaster could not begin full production until November 2027 unless Lumen draws down its existing inventory, which is only a two-week buffer. Caffè Trieste’s lead time is four weeks, but their volume limit is binding. DecafCo’s lead time for the 2028 cycle is five months, with a February 2028 delivery. The brokers can deliver within two to three weeks, but their volumes are small. If Lumen misses the 6 August 2026 deadline with Andes Direct, the entire second roaster project would be delayed by four to five months, as no other importer can supply the necessary volume in time. This date therefore governs the entire feasibility timeline.

In summary, the importers can supply the extra volume, but only if Lumen acts decisively before 7 November 2028 for the majority of the increase. Andes Direct is the key partner, capable of covering 77 percent of the projected need. Caffè Trieste and DecafCo provide niche contributions with their own timing constraints. The minor brokers can fill the remainder but introduce risk. If Lumen commits to the second roaster, the green coffee procurement plan should be finalised by late May 2027, with Andes Direct orders placed immediately, Caffè Trieste volume confirmed by end of June, and DecafCo’s 2028 allocation initiated in September 2027. This sequencing ensures that the second roaster, once installed, will have a steady green coffee supply from its first day of operation.
